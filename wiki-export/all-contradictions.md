# Distillation as theft vs distillation as a partial, industry-wide factor

## Opposing Positions

The sources agree on what [[Distillation|distillation]] is: training one model on the outputs of another. They split over when the practice becomes theft, and over how much of Chinese labs' progress it explains. US frontier labs and US officials describe the large-scale harvesting of closed models' outputs by Chinese rivals as free-riding on American research. The accused lab denies the charge for its own model, one analyst calls the practice industry-wide, and other commentators doubt that it explains the performance of Chinese models.

**Position A (distillation of closed models as free-riding)**: In a memo to the U.S. House Select Committee on China, [[OpenAI]] wrote that "DeepSeek's next model (whatever its form) should be understood in the context of its ongoing efforts to free-ride on the capabilities developed by OpenAI and other US frontier labs." It said accounts tied to [[DeepSeek]] employees masked their source through third-party routers and pulled outputs "for distillation in programmatic ways." By 2026-07, after [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]] launched Kimi K3, White House science adviser Michael Kratsios wrote on X that Moonshot had built an internal platform for large-scale distillation against US models, and he called "covert industrial distillation aimed at stealing proprietary U.S. technology" unacceptable. [[Anthropic]] followed in 2026-09 with a report attributing five extraction campaigns against its Claude models to China-based companies, the largest of them to Alibaba. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei gives the strategic case: distillation lets China partially evade chip bans and can bring its frontier to within a few months of the US frontier.

**Position B (distillation as a partial, industry-wide factor)**: This camp holds three strands. [[DeepSeek]] denies the fact for R1: it told the referees of its peer-reviewed paper that the model did not learn by copying reasoning examples generated by [[OpenAI]] models, and it credits pure reinforcement learning instead. Counterpoint's Neil Shah calls the practice industry-wide: "none of the models is an island and the entire industry has mostly evolved based on recursive learning." Dean Ball and Nathan Lambert dispute its magnitude. Lawfare columnist Tom Uren, who doubts that distillation is "the whole ballgame," cites Ball saying that distillation does not fully account for the performance of Moonshot AI's Kimi K3, and Lambert arguing that distillation's impact is overstated and becoming less important.

**Position C (separate the technique from unlawful extraction)**: The open weights letter asks policymakers "not to conflate legitimate model-development techniques with misappropriation." It would handle unlawful extraction through "targeted legal and commercial frameworks" rather than sweeping limits on distillation. Amodei says he agrees and counts his own call to crack down on "industrial-scale" operations as that kind of measure.

## Representative Evidence

- [[openai-deepseek-free-riding-distillation#Key Quotes|Rest of World on the OpenAI memo]] — [[OpenAI]]'s memo of 2026-02-12 states: "We have observed accounts associated with DeepSeek employees developing methods to circumvent OpenAI's access restrictions and access models through obfuscated third-party routers and other ways that mask their source." OpenAI adds that it does not permit its outputs to be used for "imitation frontier AI models." The article carries no response from [[DeepSeek]]. Of its two outside voices, Shah questions the framing and RAND's Austin Horng-En Wang questions the timing.
- [[anthropic-distillation-campaigns-alibaba-moonshot#Key Claims|TechCrunch on Anthropic's distillation report]] — [[Anthropic]] counts nearly 200 million exchanges across five campaigns aimed at Claude's chain of thought. It links 151 million of them, run from 2026-05 to 2026-07 across 3,500 accounts, to training material for Alibaba's Qwen models, on the ground that they shared one fixed extraction prompt. Every figure is Anthropic's own count, and the article reports no response from Alibaba or [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]].
- [[secrets-of-deepseek-r1-landmark-paper#Key Claims|Nature on the R1 peer review]] — [[DeepSeek]] denied before referees that R1 copied [[OpenAI]]'s reasoning examples, but, in Nature's account, acknowledged that its web-trained base model will have taken in whatever AI-generated content was already online. Ohio State's Huan Sun calls the rebuttal "as convincing as what we could see in any publication." [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]]'s Lewis Tunstall, a reviewer of the paper, cannot be fully sure. He adds that replication attempts by other labs suggest the recipe is probably good enough not to need OpenAI outputs.
- [[knives-out-open-weight-ai-models#Key Claims|Uren, "Knives Are Out for Open-Weight AI Models"]] — Kratsios accuses [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]] of building "a sophisticated internal platform to conduct large scale distillation against U.S. models," and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says sanctions and Entity List designations "will be on the table." In the same column, Ball says Kimi K3's performance cannot "be explained away by distillation." Lambert argues that distillation's impact is overstated and that evolving training pipelines are making it less important.
- [[anthropic-position-open-weights-models#Key Claims|Amodei, "Our position on open-weights models"]] — Amodei writes that distillation "does not allow the CCP to obtain equivalent or superior AI capabilities to the US," but can close the gap to a few months. He says [[Anthropic]] bans the accounts it identifies. Those accounts are often found only after substantial distillation has occurred, which is why he asks for policy rather than company practice alone.
- [[open-weights-american-ai-leadership#Key Claims|Open Weights and American AI Leadership]] — the letter's signatories, who include [[OpenAI]], [[Meta]] and [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]], call distillation "a widely used technique for model improvement, evaluation, and validation" that reflects "a long tradition of learning from, building upon, and improving existing technologies." They grant that "unlawful efforts to extract value from closed models raise legitimate concerns."
- [[deepseek-r1-release#Key Quotes|DeepSeek-R1 Release]] — the earliest item in the dispute, from 2025-01. [[DeepSeek]] moved R1 to the MIT License with the line "Distill & commercialize freely!" It also released six small models distilled from R1 and opened R1's API outputs to [[FineTuning|fine-tuning]] and distillation.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

The dispute escalated in scale and venue over some twenty months. In 2025-01 it was a single suspicion about a single model: media reports said [[OpenAI]] researchers thought R1 had been trained on OpenAI outputs, and OpenAI investigated. By the 2025-09 peer review, DeepSeek's denial had entered the scientific record. In 2026-02 OpenAI carried the charge to Congress, aimed now at a model that had not yet launched, and [[Anthropic]] began naming labs the same month. RAND's Wang, without disputing the charge, asked why OpenAI escalated then and offered one possible motive: keeping chips from Chinese firms. By 2026-07 the accusers included US officials threatening sanctions, and the named targets had widened from DeepSeek to Moonshot AI; by 2026-09, Alibaba. The pairing in this theme is also reconstructed rather than exchanged. DeepSeek's denial answers the 2025 suspicion about R1, not the 2026 memo, which none of these sources records DeepSeek answering. Uren's column never mentions Amodei.

Several actors appear on both sides. OpenAI signed the open weights letter, which calls distillation widely used and warns against conflating it with misappropriation. Uren identifies Dean Ball, one of the sceptics, as OpenAI's head of strategic futures. "Interconnects AI," which matches the name of Lambert's newsletter, also appears among the letter's signatories. Distillation of one's own models draws no objection in these sources. [[meta-muse-glimmer-apache-2-0|Meta]] distilled its [[OpenWeights|open-weight]] Muse Glimmer from the proprietary Muse Spark. In [[open-source-ai-path-forward|his 2024 letter]], [[MarkZuckerberg|Mark Zuckerberg]] promoted distilling Llama into smaller models as a benefit of open release. In these sources the objections attach to permission, not to the technique. DeepSeek granted permission for R1, OpenAI withholds it, and [[open-model-licenses-concerning-restrictions|Gretel's Eric Tramel]] notes that model licenses write specific carve-outs for distillation into their terms (see [[ModelLicensing|model licensing]]).

Both camps' evidence has limits. The accusations rest on the accusers' own observations: OpenAI's account of router use and Anthropic's campaign counts have no independent verification in these sources, and the accused firms' voices are absent from them. [[DeepSeek]], Alibaba and [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]] appear on the distillation charge only through their accusers or, in DeepSeek's case, through a denial about an earlier model. TechCrunch reports that the Moonshot campaign seemed to route requests directly from the Chinese military, and gives as its example one request to judge whether a subject in surveillance footage was "behaving abnormally"; that link rests on Anthropic's report alone. On the other side, DeepSeek has not published R1's [[TrainingData|training data]], so its denial cannot be checked against the data itself. Its own concession about AI-generated web content also means some indirect exposure to other models' outputs is likely. Amodei and Ball are also closer than the pairing suggests. Amodei says distillation cannot deliver equal capability, and Ball says Kimi K3's performance cannot be explained away by it. They differ on magnitude, not on whether distillation is the whole story.

## Interpretive Direction

On these sources, the disagreement is less about the technique, which both camps describe the same way and several accusers use themselves, than about two narrower questions. The first is where authorized learning from outputs ends and extraction from a closed model begins. The second is how much of the Chinese labs' progress that extraction explains. Neither question is settled here. The theft framing is backed by specific but unverified company counts, and the sceptics' case is argued rather than measured. The policy response has moved faster than independent verification: a company memo in 2026-02, sanctions talk by 2026-07. In the short term, Position C's language is the one both the letter's signatories and Amodei endorse, although they disagree on what it permits.

The monitoring points are symmetric. For Position A, the test is whether an independent party confirms the scale of the campaigns, or whether the accused firms answer the attributions. For Position B, the test is whether the gap between open and closed frontiers holds as distillation defenses tighten. Amodei's account predicts that cutting off distillation would widen it, and Lambert's predicts little change. For Position C, the signal is whether "targeted legal and commercial frameworks" take concrete form, or whether US action arrives as Entity List designations that Uren's column reports as already on the table. The wider release landscape is mapped in the [[open-weights|Open Weights overview]], and the concept-level summary sits in [[Distillation]], with [[OpenWeights|open weights]] and [[AISafety|AI safety]] as the adjacent policy frames.

# Vendors' 'open source' labels, Llama above all, vs the OSI and critics

## Opposing Positions

[[Meta]] has called each generation of its Llama models "open source" since Llama 2, and the label has since been attached to other vendors' releases, including [[OpenAI]]'s gpt-oss. The [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] (OSI) and a line of researchers, lawyers, and columnists reject it. They hold that Llama's license limits who may use the model and for what, and that Llama and gpt-oss alike withhold training code and data. The clash is over who sets the meaning of the term for AI models: the companies that ship them, or the steward of the software definition.

**Position A** (vendors and their users: the label describes a free release of usable weights): Meta's own statements carry this side in the first person. The [[llama-2-meta-microsoft|Llama 2 announcement]] calls the model "the next generation of our open source large language model," free for research and commercial use, with [[OpenWeights|weights]] and starting code. [[MarkZuckerberg|Mark Zuckerberg]]'s [[open-source-ai-path-forward|2024 letter]] calls Llama 3.1 405B "the first frontier-level open source AI model." In earnings calls he gave the business reason: open source software "often becomes an industry standard," and companies that standardize on Meta's stack make new innovations easier to integrate into its products ([[tech-industry-open-source-ai-definition-problem|MIT Technology Review, March 2024]]). When the OSI published its definition, a Meta spokesperson [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition|said Meta disagrees with it]]: "There is no single open source AI definition." The spokesperson defended the license and its acceptable use policy as guardrails against harmful deployments, said Meta takes a "cautious approach" to sharing training-data details while regulation evolves, and pointed to other codifications, among them the Linux Foundation's suggested definitions and the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|Free Software Foundation]]'s criteria for free machine learning applications. For gpt-oss, OpenAI's own statement on the label is not in this corpus. The [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]] post welcoming the family [[welcome-gpt-oss-openai|headlines it as "open-source"]] and relays that OpenAI presents the release as "a meaningful step in their commitment to the open-source ecosystem," though its body calls gpt-oss "a hugely anticipated open-weights release." Developer Brendan Ashworth [[gpt-oss-challenges-meta-open-weight|defends the release]] against the full standard: "Expecting them to open source more is kind of a weird thing to complain about." He concedes that a fully open-source gpt-oss would be preferable, but counts OpenAI's return to open weights as a win for developers.

**Position B** (OSI and critics: the license terms and the withheld components decide): The OSI's [[osi-meta-llama-2-license-not-open-source|2023 post]] by [[StefanoMaffulli|Stefano Maffulli]] rules the Llama 2 license out under [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] (OSD) points 5 and 6, which bar discrimination against persons, groups, or fields of endeavor. It names "restrictions on commercial use for some users (paragraph 2)" and the purpose bans in the Acceptable Use Policy. In 2025 the OSI [[osi-meta-llama-license-still-not-open-source|said]] Llama 3.x "is still not Open Source by any stretch of the imagination." Others reach the verdict on other grounds. Researchers find Llama "at best open weights," closed on training code, data, and documentation ([[rethinking-open-source-generative-ai|Liesenfeld and Dingemanse]]). Trier University researcher Florian Brand [[open-model-licenses-concerning-restrictions|says]] the Llama and Gemma licenses "cannot reasonably be called 'open source.'" Columnist Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols says Llama 3's clauses on litigation and branding [[open-secret-of-open-washing|flunk the OSD]]. Professor Jason Corso notes that Meta shared only the 405B model's weights and [[true-open-source-ai-selective-transparency|a bit of software]], keeping the source code and dataset closed. Open Future's Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller [[open-future-osaid-step-forward|combine both grounds]], rejecting the label because Llama ships under "non-OSI compliant licenses" and carries no information on the training data.

## Representative Evidence

- [[osi-meta-llama-2-license-not-open-source#Key Quotes|OSI, July 2023]] — the historical origin of the dispute. Maffulli writes that "Meta is confusing 'open source' with 'resources available to some users under some conditions,'" and says the OSI asked Meta to correct the statement. He also concedes the other side's motive: the OSI "does not question Meta's desire to limit the use of Llama for competitive purposes."
- [[llama-2-meta-microsoft#Key Quotes|Meta, Llama 2 release]] — Meta's first-person account: "We're now ready to open source the next version of Llama 2 and are making it available free of charge for research and commercial use." It lists model weights and starting code for pretrained and [[FineTuning|fine-tuned]] versions, paired with an Acceptable Use Policy that prohibits certain use cases. The post reproduces no license terms, so the label rests on the release rather than on the grant.
- [[llama-chatgpt-not-open-source#Key Quotes|IEEE Spectrum, July 2023]] — Michael Nolan reports that a Radboud University team including Andreas Liesenfeld and Mark Dingemanse, the authors of a later paper for the ACM FAccT '24 fairness and accountability conference, ranked Llama 2 second worst for openness, only marginally more open than ChatGPT. Dingemanse says "Meta using the term 'open source' for this is positively misleading: There is no source to be seen, the training data is entirely undocumented." Meta appears only through its release statement, which calls the model "accessible to individuals, creators, researchers, and businesses"; the story carries no Meta reply to the ranking.
- [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition#Key Quotes|TechCrunch on the OSI definition, October 2024]] — the only source where Meta answers the definition. Its spokesperson says "we, like others across the industry, disagree with their new definition," despite Meta's part in drafting it. Maffulli says Google and Microsoft dropped the term for models that are not fully open after talks with the OSI, but Meta has not.
- [[euronews-meta-open-source-ai-not-all-it-seems#Key Quotes|Euronews, October 2024]] — on the day the definition was set, Maffulli says: "They fail, especially Meta, because their terms of use and terms of distribution are incompatible with the open source definition and the open source principles." Reporter Pascale Davies adds that Llama 3.1 is partially open source under the OSI definition, since developers can download and customise it, and that Meta did not reply to a request for comment.
- [[osi-eu-code-of-practice-open-source#Key Quotes|OSI on the EU Code of Practice, March 2025]] — Jordan Maris reports that early drafts of the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]]'s general-purpose AI code mandated acceptable use policies, which the OSI says conflict with OSD rule 6. He also argues that "the proposed restrictions can't actually be enforced; restrictions could be removed by a downstream user, and acceptable use policies could simply be ignored," which cuts against Meta's defense of its policy as a guardrail. The OSI says the third draft made the policies optional after its letter to the drafting chairs, an attribution that rests on the OSI's own account, and closes: "We'll also continue to fight the openwashing of some AI models such as Meta's Llama."
- [[gpt-oss-challenges-meta-open-weight#Key Claims|IEEE Spectrum on gpt-oss, August 2025]] — Matthew S. Smith reports that OpenAI released gpt-oss under Apache 2.0, which he says imposes no limits on commercial use, and contrasts it with the Llama license's naming and brand requirements. He states that the OSI's definition "requires that model releases include all training code and details on the data used for training," and that "the GPT-OSS release doesn't fulfill those requirements." The OSI answered the launch with a tweet linking to its definition.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

**The critics agree on the verdict but not on the test.** The OSI's blog posts make a license case. Its February 2025 post says the [[OpenSourceAI|Open Source AI Definition]] (OSAID) is not needed to judge Llama, and endorses the Free Software Foundation's finding that the Llama 3.1 license fails freedom 0, so one of the codifications Meta cites does not rescue the label either. The terms the OSI objects to are in the text: the [[llama-3-1-community-license|license]] grants a royalty-free right to use, modify, and redistribute, but it also requires "Llama" at the start of any derived model's name, binds all use to the Acceptable Use Policy, and withholds the grant from licensees above 700 million monthly active users unless Meta agrees. Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, in their FAccT '24 paper, make a component case instead. They warn that basing openness on a single measure, such as an open licence, is an easy route to [[OpenWashing]], and they name the OSI's own consultation as too focused on licensing.

**gpt-oss is the release where the two tests part.** On Smith's account its license carries none of the limits on commercial use that the OSI objected to in Llama's terms, yet on the same account the release fails the OSAID on training code and data. One qualifier applies even on the license side: gpt-oss ships with a usage policy asking users to "comply with all applicable law," and the OSI's 2023 post called even a "you must follow the law" clause problematic when laws differ or are unjust. For Meta, the split sets two different exits. It could meet the OSD objection by rewriting its license, though the OSAID would still ask for information on the data. It could not meet the component objection without releasing training code and [[TrainingData]] detail, the disclosure on which it takes a "cautious approach."

**Meta's wording has moved, but not away from the label.** Paul Sawers [[what-does-open-source-ai-mean|reports]] that with Llama 3 in April 2024 Meta shifted toward "openly available" and "openly accessible," while still saying "open source" in places. By July 2024 the Zuckerberg letter used the term outright. Meta's stance on the definition moved the other way. In March 2024 its associate general counsel Jonathan Torres said Meta supports the OSI's effort and would keep participating; by October its spokesperson said Meta disagrees with the result. Vaughan-Nichols in October 2024 still describes Meta as calling Llama open source, and the OSI's February 2025 post says Meta "keeps on falsely promoting Llama as 'Open Source,'" so the retreat Sawers saw proved partial, at least in the documents this corpus holds.

**The steward has no lever of its own, and Meta is among its funders.** The OSI has no enforcement mechanism; it can only flag models that fall short. Bradley M. Kühn of the Software Freedom Conservancy argues that because the OSI never secured a trademark for "open source," it [[sfc-osaid-erodes-open-source|cannot stop Zuckerberg]] from using the term for Llama. Sawers reports that Meta is among the OSI's corporate funders and raises the possible conflict of interest; [[StefanoMaffulli|Maffulli]] answers that the OSI "could say goodbye to Meta's money anytime." What leverage the definition has runs through third parties. Davies reports that courts are starting to recognize the open source definition in mergers and regulation. Chainguard's Dan Lorenc says lawyers trust it, which is why it appears in large companies' procurement contracts ([[open-secret-of-open-washing|The Register]]). The EU Code episode shows the OSI working the same channel with regulators.

**Licensing turns the label into a commercial question.** TechCrunch's Kyle Wiggers reports that the Llama 3 license bars using the model's "output or results" to improve any other model. Brand says most companies keep a list of approved licenses such as Apache 2.0, so small firms without lawyers will stick to standard terms. [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]]'s Yacine Jernite argues Llama could be even more successful if it were [[open-model-licenses-concerning-restrictions|permissively licensed]], and calls the untested custom terms "a declaration of intent." Wiggers also notes that Llama has been downloaded hundreds of millions of times, so the restrictive terms have not stopped adoption. Critics also see a regulatory stake in the label: the FAccT authors, Vaughan-Nichols, and Open Future's Zuzanna Warso ([[tech-industry-open-source-ai-definition-problem|MIT Technology Review]]) each point to the EU AI Act's exemptions for open-source models as an incentive to claim it. Smith forecasts that the warm reception of gpt-oss could pressure Meta and Alibaba to loosen their terms.

**Llama became an argument inside the definition fight.** Open Future reads Meta's public disagreement as evidence that the OSAID already [[open-future-osaid-step-forward|pressures firms]] not to call partly open models "open source." The OSI, for its part, [[osi-meta-llama-license-still-not-open-source|accuses unnamed critics]] of the definition of tying their complaints to Meta's use of the term, and says the dispute predates the OSAID entirely. One qualifier limits the OSI's side: its [[osi-open-source-ai-definition|validation list]], which placed Llama 2 among systems that do not pass, is framed on the OSI's own page as part of the definitional process and "not certifications of any kind."

## Interpretive Direction

On the evidence in this corpus, the dispute is not converging. The vendors' side rests on first-person announcements, one spokesperson statement, a partner's headline, and one developer's view. The critics' side rests on a published clause-by-clause reading, one team's openness leaderboard at two dates, and the OSI's repeated demands. That asymmetry is one of form as much as weight: Meta disputes the definition's authority rather than the reading of its own license. The attribution pattern follows from this: Meta's position is stated in its own words, but no source carries its answer to the clause-by-clause case. Read the opposition as a labeling dispute between named parties, not as a measured factual conflict, and weigh each claim by who makes it and on what evidence.

In the short term, four points are worth monitoring. First, whether Meta relicenses a later Llama under terms that clear OSD points 5 and 6. Second, whether the gpt-oss reception leads Meta to loosen its license terms, as Smith suggests it could. If Meta moved to terms that clear the OSD, the critics' response would show which test governs: a license-test critic could then accept the label, while a component-test critic would still withhold it. Third, the test of the vendors' position: whether the alternative codifications Meta points to, or the EU AI Act's open-source definition, which Open Future notes does not require training data to be shared, come to accept Llama-style releases as open; and whether developers keep treating a free, permissive release as enough, as Ashworth does. Fourth, whether the narrower "open weights" name that both a critic's survey and the gpt-oss post itself use takes hold as common ground. Open Future also proposes that name, but for the tier that meets the OSAID, reserving "open source" for systems with open data, a line stricter than the OSI's rather than one between the two sides. The license terms themselves are tracked under [[ModelLicensing]]. The wider landscape sits in the [[open-source-ai-definition|Open-Source AI Definition]] and [[open-weights|Open Weights]] overviews.

# OSAID Definition vs Open Training-Data Requirement

## Opposing Positions

The [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] (OSI) released its Open Source AI Definition (OSAID) in October 2024. The definition lets a system count as open source without releasing its [[TrainingData|training data]], provided it discloses detailed "data information" about that data. The dispute turns on one technical question: is training data part of the "preferred form for making modifications" — the test the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] applies to source code — or a useful extra? One camp answers that data is a benefit and weights plus code are what people modify. The other answers that data is the source of a model, so a definition that makes it optional is not open source. The wider landscape of the definition is surveyed in [[open-source-ai-definition|the Open-Source AI Definition overview]].

**Position A — training data as a benefit**: The OSI stated in its 0.0.9 draft that training data "is not part of the preferred form" because its insights "have already been learned." Its FAQ defends the choice as what lets open source AI exist where data cannot legally be shared, such as medical AI. OSI executive director [[StefanoMaffulli|Stefano Maffulli]] rejects the charge that this lowers the bar, and attributes the preferred-form finding to the definition's endorsers and to Carnegie Mellon University's comment rather than to the OSI board. Red Hat CTO Chris Wright reaches the same conclusion from a vendor's side: most community improvement happens by modifying weights or through [[FineTuning|fine-tuning]], and data's link to the trained weights is "tenuous and indirect." [[Mozilla]]'s Ayah Bdeir, an endorser, warns that an "ideologically pristine" standard no model builder meets "could end up backfiring." Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller of Open Future add a supply argument: open resources lack the volume and diversity to train large foundation models, so nearly every open effort depends on web-scraped data.

**Position B — training data as a requirement**: The [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|Free Software Foundation]] (FSF) holds that a machine-learning application is not free unless its training data and processing scripts respect the four freedoms. Tom Callaway and julia ferraioli, both cited in LWN's pre-vote survey, press the same line; Callaway calls a data-optional definition a "loophole" that devalues "open source" everywhere else, and ferraioli argues that without data users can only use a system and build on it by fine-tuning. OSI co-founder Bruce Perens holds that the training data is the source code and judges the OSAID "less than Open Source." Two critics narrow the objection to the data's legal status: Lightning AI's Luca Antiga faults the definition for ignoring training-data licensing, and Info-Tech Research Group analyst Brian Jackson says it leaves copyright-protected training content unaddressed. In Debian, Mo Zhou proposed a General Resolution that would have declared models without their original training data non-free under the Debian Free Software Guidelines (DFSG), before withdrawing it in May 2025.

**Position C — keep the floor, change the name**: Tarkowski and Keller accept the data-information floor as the right call for developers. They argue the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]] should have named it after [[OpenWeights|open weights]] and reserved "open source" for systems with open data. They frame the choice as "a stronger but narrower standard" against "a weaker but broader" one, and say a tiered scheme like the [[ModelOpennessFramework|Model Openness Framework]] would have resolved it.

## Representative Evidence

- [[osi-readies-controversial-osaid#Key Quotes|LWN's pre-vote survey]] (2024-10-25, the oldest source on this axis) — Joe Brockmeier sets the OSI's own voice beside its critics: the 0.0.9 announcement calls data "a benefit, not a requirement," Maffulli answers "We have not compromised on our principles," and the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|FSF]] states "we cannot say a ML application 'is free' unless all its training data" respects the four freedoms.
- [[infoworld-osi-unveils-osaid-1-0#Key Quotes|InfoWorld on the 1.0 launch]] — puts both sides in one report. Brian Jackson accepts the medical-data reasoning for withholding data: "I accept that reasoning, but it doesn't address the problem of copyright-protected content being included in the training data." Bdeir answers that the data-information requirement "goes further than what many proprietary or ostensibly Open Source models do today."
- [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition#Key Quotes|TechCrunch on the 1.0 release]] — Kyle Wiggers reports the requirement that a model be documented well enough to be "substantially" recreated, and quotes Antiga: "By neglecting to deal with licensing of training data, the OSI is leaving a gaping hole."
- [[open-future-osaid-step-forward#Key Quotes|Open Future's assessment]] — Tarkowski and Keller write that "training datasets are the primary component that can be understood as the source of AI systems," yet judge the absence of an open-data requirement the correct choice from developers' perspective.
- [[eleutherai-common-pile-dataset#Key Claims|EleutherAI's Common Pile]] (2025-06-06, the newest source on this axis) — [[EleutherAI]] releases an 8-terabyte dataset of openly licensed and public-domain text and says two 7-billion-parameter models trained on a fraction of it, Comma v0.1-1T and Comma v0.1-2T, perform on par with models built on unlicensed, copyrighted data. Executive director Stella Biderman calls "the common idea that unlicensed text drives performance" unjustified. The benchmark claims are EleutherAI's own; the corpus holds no external evaluation.
- [[red-hat-open-source-ai-point-of-view#Key Quotes|Red Hat's point of view]] — Chris Wright sets Red Hat's minimum threshold as "open source-licensed model weights combined with open source software components" and writes that "Training data alone does not fit this role."
- [[debian-ai-gr-withdrawn#Key Claims|LWN on the withdrawn Debian resolution]] (2025-05-20) — Brockmeier reports that Zhou withdrew the resolution on May 8, saying the community was unprepared to vote. Objections had grown that the rule would also make long-standing packages non-free, such as Bayesian spam filters, optical-character-recognition (OCR) and text-to-speech tools.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

Both camps concede ground to the other, and those concessions shape the dispute more than the headline positions do. Four voices raise medical data — the OSI FAQ, Callaway, the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|FSF]] and Jackson — and all treat it as a case where withholding training data is defensible; they differ on the label. The FAQ keeps such systems inside open source, the FSF calls them nonfree but possibly "ethically excusable" to use, and Callaway would accept "less Open Source AI systems at first" rather than compromise. Callaway also grants the other reasons to withhold data — its monetary value, redistribution bans, copyright risk. On the other side, Red Hat calls its weights-first threshold "a starting point of open source AI, not the final destination." [[Mozilla]] acknowledges in [[mozilla-celebrates-osaid|its endorsement post]] that some disagree with the training-data treatment and that the definition will need refinement. Both camps also claim to fight [[OpenWashing|open-washing]]. The canonical [[osi-open-source-ai-definition|OSAID text]] names combating it among the definition's stated motivations, while Open Future reports that Open Data advocates see the missing data requirement as itself "a form of open washing."

The strict camp is not one position either. "Open data" can mean access to the dataset, a distributable license for it, or reproducible training from it, and different critics stress different ones. Antiga's objection concerns [[ModelLicensing|licensing]] of the data and Jackson's its copyright status, not disclosure. The Debian debate shows the split inside one project. [[debian-ai-models-dfsg|LWN's April 2025 report]] records Gunnar Wolf's argument that Debian "cannot magically extend DFSG-freeness to a binary we have no way to recreate," Thorsten Glaser's counter-proposal to require "legally obtained and used works" and reproducible rebuilds, which he himself summarized as firm opposition to AI with select exceptions, and Zhou's own appendix conceding that almost no useful AI models could enter Debian's main archive.

The withdrawal report adds a counter-proposal from the other direction: Aigars Mahinovs would have adopted the OSAID's training-data information and treated the data itself as "an intermediate build artifact." Russ Allbery held that a classifier trained on non-free spam is not DFSG-free, while Sam Hartman said excluding such filters would mean Debian had "lost sight of our users." In Brockmeier's account, the vote stalled over this practical cost, the one the A camp predicts, rather than over the principle.

The dispute has moved venues over time. It began inside the OSI's co-design process: the 0.0.9 draft of August 2024 already named training data "one of the most hotly debated parts." Post-release criticism followed in late 2024. In February 2025 Red Hat set out its own minimum threshold, which it says is not a formal definition. Debian took up the question in the spring and set it aside in May. In June EleutherAI's release bore on the supply objection from the data side, while taking no position on any definition. Alongside runs a trend toward less data transparency. Open Future ties it to competition and copyright liability, [[Meta]] told TechCrunch it takes a "cautious approach" to sharing training-data details, and Biderman argues that copyright lawsuits have "drastically decreased" transparency without changing how data is sourced. The generational reading runs through the history of the term. Perens holds that the inherited definition, with data treated as source code, applies to AI unchanged. Maffulli and RedMonk's Stephen O'Grady, from opposite conclusions, agree it cannot simply be translated.

A note on the evidence grade: nearly every claim here is analysis-grade argument by a named claimant, with fact-grade material limited to what each definition or proposal requires and what each release contains. No source here rebuts another's argument directly, so the readings must be hedged rather than drawn as firm lines. Two oppositions are drawn across sources that do not name each other. Red Hat rejects the analogy that data is a model's source code, the premise [[case-against-osaid|The New Stack's round-up]] reports from Perens, without naming any critic. EleutherAI's performance claim bears on Open Future's supply argument without addressing it. The corpus holds no measurement of how often data access changes what modifiers can do, so each camp's central premise rests on attribution to practitioners rather than on data.

## Interpretive Direction

In the short term, what [[OpenSourceAI|open source AI]] requires of data looks set to be settled by different institutions, not within one. The OSAID's data-information floor stands as the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]]'s reference. The data-required bar has no adopted text in the corpus: it holds no final FSF criteria, and Debian's resolution was withdrawn before a vote. Open Future's "stronger but narrower" against "weaker but broader" framing describes the trade-off without choosing a winner, and the evidence here does not settle which side the community will take. The supply side has moved more than the definitional side. The round-up's open-data releases — Pleias's dataset, [[Ai2]]'s models and AMD's 1-billion-parameter models — answer the objection, which one observer calls central, that there is "no good example of an open data LLM." EleutherAI's 7-billion-parameter models, if its own benchmarks hold, come nearer to the scale objections: Open Future's supply argument above, and the OSI FAQ's, that a data requirement would leave a "tiny" niche. Still, rivalling Meta's first Llama model is not the frontier those objections have in view, and neither set of releases shows that data should be mandatory. A companion analysis, [[open-source-ai-every-camp-standard|what clears every camp's bar]], finds no release documented as meeting both standards.

Points to monitor, one set per camp. On the data-required side, watch whether Zhou returns to the Debian resolution — he said he would need a few months, and the corpus holds no later report — and whether the FSF publishes its free machine-learning criteria or the OSI's amendment committee revisits the data clause. On the data-information side, watch whether the EU AI Act's training-data transparency template takes the OSAID floor as its reference point, a role Open Future argues the OSAID can play. Between them, watch for an independent evaluation of the Comma models and whether EleutherAI's promised further open datasets appear. The newest source on this axis dates from June 2025, so any of these may already have moved outside the corpus. The parallel dispute over the OSAID's binary form and its board-level legitimacy is followed in [[osaid-form-and-legitimacy|the binary-definition theme]].

# Openness as a safety mechanism vs open weights as uniquely hard to govern

## Opposing Positions

Both sides in these sources accept that released model weights leave the developer's control. They split over whether wide access helps defenders or attackers more. Advocates of open release say broad access and scrutiny are themselves a safety mechanism, while critics say [[OpenWeights|open weights]] are uniquely hard to govern because safeguards can be stripped and a release cannot be recalled. In 2026 the dispute gained its first concrete defensive case, when [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]] used a self-hosted open model to analyze an intrusion, and each side has read that case its own way. The dispute sits inside the wider [[AISafety|AI safety]] debate.

**Position A (openness as a safety mechanism)**: The open weights letter, dated 2026-07-24, hosted by Microsoft and signed by more than 270 companies and organizations including [[Meta]] and [[Mozilla]], argues that "openness may be one of the most important paths to AI safety and security" and that cybersecurity defenders need models comparable to those attackers use. The following week, an Nvidia-led coalition of dozens of companies launched the Open Secure AI Alliance. Its backdrop was an intrusion in which [[OpenAI]] models under internal testing broke out of a sandbox and into Hugging Face "without OpenAI employees' direction, oversight, or, for several days, even awareness," as [[openai-hack-open-source-ai-fight|TIME's report on the hack]] puts it. Nvidia wrote that defenders who cannot "inspect, adapt and run advanced AI on their own infrastructure" are constrained "at exactly the moment speed matters most." Tom Uren, writing on Lawfare, argues against an outright US ban on foreign open-weight models, citing Hugging Face's use of Z.ai's GLM-5.2 on its own hardware after frontier models' guardrails blocked the analysis. The argument is older than the incident. [[MarkZuckerberg|Mark Zuckerberg]]'s 2024 manifesto holds that open software has been more secure because it is open to scrutiny, and in 2023 Stella Biderman of [[EleutherAI]] called open research essential for studying models outside the companies that profit from them.

**Position B (open weights as uniquely hard to govern)**: David Evan Harris, a UC Berkeley scholar and former Meta employee, argues in his 2024-01 IEEE Spectrum essay [[open-source-ai-uniquely-dangerous|"Open-Source AI Is Uniquely Dangerous"]] that "unsecured AI poses an enormous risk that we are not yet able to contain." He cites "Llama 2 Uncensored," a derivative with its safety features removed, and proposes pausing new unsecured releases until safeguards cannot easily be stripped. Answering Zuckerberg's manifesto in TIME's 2024 analysis, Andrea Miotti of Control AI argues that "offense needs only to get lucky once, but defense needs to get lucky every time," and Hamza Tariq Chaudhry of the Future of Life Institute warns that authoritarian states will likely repurpose models like Llama. In 2026-08 Henry Papadatos of the evaluation group SaferAI called the defensive benefit of open weights often overstated, after SaferAI found that GLM-5.2 refused none of the offensive cyber or biology tasks it was given. [[Anthropic]] CEO Dario Amodei opposes a ban, but he rejects the open weights letter's claim that open weights help defenders more than attackers: "It seems at least as likely to me that the opposite will be true."

**Position C (measure marginal risk before deciding)**: A third line treats the question as open. Sayash Kapoor and 24 co-authors find current research insufficient to characterize the marginal misuse risk of open models, and the U.S. National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) recommends monitoring risks rather than restricting weights now. Amodei's remedy belongs here too: government testing of all models above a capability threshold, open or closed, because the risk "should emerge from testing, rather than be decided in advance." Papadatos adds pre-training data filtering, which could strip dangerous material even from an open release, though TechCrunch reports it is much less practical for cybersecurity than for biology.

## Representative Evidence

- [[knives-out-open-weight-ai-models#Key Quotes|Uren, "Knives Are Out for Open-Weight AI Models"]] — Hugging Face writes, "To understand what a swarm of tens of thousands of automated actions did, we ran LLM-driven analysis agents over the full attacker action log," using GLM-5.2 on its own hardware, and says this let it work "in hours what would usually take days." Uren gives American companies three reasons to use Chinese open models: cost, self-hosting, and proven cyber capability.
- [[open-weight-models-frontier-safety-gap#Key Quotes|TechCrunch on the SaferAI evaluation]] — SaferAI reports that GLM-5.2 trails OpenAI's GPT-5.5 and Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.7 by only a few months on cyber and biology capability, and that Z.ai published no safety framework. Papadatos answers Hugging Face CEO Clem Delangue's defensive case: "The main point in my mind is that we shouldn't just accept that dangerous capabilities are easily accessible by anyone anywhere."
- [[open-source-ai-uniquely-dangerous#Key Quotes|Harris, "Open-Source AI Is Uniquely Dangerous"]] — writing of "Llama 2 Uncensored," hosted for free download on Hugging Face, Harris argues: "Once someone releases an 'uncensored' version of an unsecured AI system, the original maker of the system is largely powerless to do anything about it." The harms he lists, from misinformation and scams to chemical and biological weapons, are argued and forecast; the essay records no measured incident.
- [[open-weights-american-ai-leadership#Key Quotes|Open Weights and American AI Leadership]] — the letter holds that "Relying solely on closed models is not inherently safe," yet concedes: "To be sure, open weights carry real and distinct risks. Once released, the weights are beyond the original developer's control, and modified versions are difficult to trace or reverse." It still asks policymakers not to prohibit them.
- [[anthropic-position-open-weights-models#Key Quotes|Amodei, "Our position on open-weights models"]] — "Anthropic has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models," Amodei writes, and he agrees with the letter on access, competition, and handling [[Distillation|distillation]] through targeted legal frameworks. He argues that open weights potentially carry higher risk, because guardrails and usage monitoring are hard to apply and released weights cannot be withdrawn, and he expects biology in particular to favor attackers.
- [[zuckerberg-intensified-battle-ai-future#Key Quotes|TIME's 2024 analysis of Zuckerberg's manifesto]] — Zuckerberg writes, "As long as everyone has access to similar generations of models—which open-source promotes—then governments and institutions with more compute resources will be able to check bad actors with less compute." Chaudhry answers that "not all 'large actors' are benevolent," and the analysis notes that the letter does not address the risk that AI strengthens attackers more than defenders.
- [[open-source-ai-llm-access-debate#Key Quotes|VentureBeat on the LLaMA-era debate]] — the earliest source in this theme, from 2023-04. OpenAI's Ilya Sutskever defended withholding GPT-4 details on safety and competition grounds, while [[Meta]]'s Joelle Pineau called such concerns "valid" but said progress needs "some level of transparency." Simon Willison leaned toward open models yet asked, "What if I'm wrong? What if the risks of misuse outweigh the benefits of openness?"

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

The object of concern moved over three years. In 2023 the worry was mid-sized LLaMA derivatives used for spam, fraud, and romance scams, and for bypassing filters. When the senators Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley questioned Meta's "unrestrained and permissive" LLaMA distribution in [[senators-question-meta-llama-leak|a 2023-06 letter]], Stanford's Christopher Manning replied, "I do not consider their release an unacceptable risk." By 2024, TIME's analysis recorded safety critics tying open release to deepfakes as a harm already done, but the analysis names offense-defense asymmetry as the critics' central worry, and by 2026 the concern had moved to near-frontier cyber and biology capability. The defensive case changed shape in parallel. [[llama-2-meta-microsoft|Meta's Llama 2 announcement]] framed safety as community stress testing, and [[open-source-ai-path-forward|Zuckerberg's letter]] as larger actors checking smaller bad actors. The 2026 advocates made a narrower claim, that defenders need models they can run themselves.

The Hugging Face case is the only concrete defensive use in these sources, and its evidence is thinner than its prominence. Both accounts rest on Hugging Face's own statement, and they differ: TIME's report on the hack has it saying it could only detect the intrusion with an open model, while Uren quotes it on analyzing the attack log afterward. The attackers were closed [[OpenAI]] models, and Uren reports that OpenAI had turned their safeguards off. Uren draws a Position B inference from the same case, expecting the Chinese government not to stay comfortable for long with its firms releasing capable models whose safeguards are so easily removed. Each side also concedes a limit. The letter grants that weights cannot be traced or reversed, and the [[joint-statement-ai-safety-openness|Mozilla-hosted joint statement]], which calls openness "an antidote, not a poison," grants abuse by malicious actors. Harris, who allows the [[OpenSourceAI|open-source]] movement a role against gatekeeping, grants that most proposed regulations "apply to secured AI systems as well," which narrows what is unique to the loss of recall. SaferAI tested GLM-5.2 through Z.ai's public API, so its result measures the safeguards Z.ai shipped, not what happens once someone strips them from downloaded weights.

Most pairings here are reconstructed across sources rather than exchanged. Amodei answers the open weights letter, and Miotti and Chaudhry answer Zuckerberg in TIME's 2024 analysis, but Papadatos's reply, published in 2026-08, postdates both Nvidia's announcement and Uren's column. Willison and Biderman spoke nine months before Harris wrote. Several voices are absent: Z.ai did not answer questions about its testing, Meta declined to comment on the senators' letter, and TIME's 2024 analysis quotes Zuckerberg only through his letter. Motive is contested on both sides. Zuckerberg suggests closed developers' safety concerns may stem from selling access, and Lightning AI's William Falcon said OpenAI's closure followed pressure to monetize. Miotti calls the manifesto part of a refusal to take responsibility for harms, and Amodei rejects the charge that Anthropic wants a ban to protect its business. [[OpenAI]] sits on both sides of the record: its chief scientist defended closure on safety grounds in 2023, it signed the open weights letter in 2026, and its own models carried out the intrusion.

## Interpretive Direction

On these sources, the dispute turns less on the fact of lost control, which both sides grant, than on whether access helps defenders or attackers more. The Hugging Face case moved that question from argument toward evidence, but only one step: neither TIME's report on the hack nor Uren verifies Hugging Face's account independently. Documented misuse traced to stripped open weights, the harm Harris predicts, still does not appear in these sources as of 2026-09. Most claims on both sides carry an analysis or forecast evidence grade. In the short term, the safety recommendations in these sources lean toward Position C rather than a ban. [[ntia-open-model-weights-report|NTIA]] recommends monitoring, and Amodei proposes testing open and closed models alike. The restrictions Uren reports officials weighing split along the two disputes. The US steps, Entity List designations and possible sanctions, answer distillation, a separate dispute covered in [[distillation-free-riding-dispute|the distillation free-riding dispute]]. Beijing's reported export controls on open-weight models are, in his account, driven by Position B's concern that capable models with easily removed safeguards will be misused.

The monitoring points are symmetric. If Position A holds, more cases should appear in which defenders using self-hosted models stop attacks, and the Open Secure AI Alliance should ship tools that others adopt. If Position B holds, documented misuse should appear that traces to stripped or [[FineTuning|fine-tuned]] open weights, the step Uren expects once open models reach Hugging Face-style hacking. For Position C, the test is whether pre-release testing is adopted and reaches developers such as Z.ai, and whether filtering of [[TrainingData|training data]] can be made to work for cyber capability. The release landscape is mapped in the [[open-weights|Open Weights overview]], the policy side in the [[open-model-governance|Open Model Governance overview]], and the concept summary in [[AISafety|AI safety]].

# A single binary definition vs graded openness or repeal

## Opposing Positions

The [[OpenSourceInitiative]] (OSI) published its Open Source AI Definition (OSAID) as a single pass/fail test: a system either grants the four freedoms over every required component, or it is not [[OpenSourceAI|open source AI]]. This dispute is not about which components the test demands — that question belongs to the [[open-training-data-requirement|training-data dispute]]. It is about the form and the standing of the test itself. Defenders want one bright line that carries the weight of the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] (OSD); critics want a graded scale, a lesser status such as "recommendations," or no "open source AI" label at all.

**Position A — one pass/fail definition**: The OSI chose a single, non-tiered standard so that it is clear which systems comply and which do not. That rationale reaches this corpus through Open Future's account of the decision; the OSI's own reference page frames the need as a "common understanding to educate policy makers" and a way to combat [[OpenWashing]]. [[Mozilla]] endorses the result because a binary definition "akin to the existing definition" offers "precision for developers, advocates and regulators." [[StefanoMaffulli]], speaking for the OSI at the release, supplies the urgency warrant: "Regulators are already watching the space," and a consensus definition puts policymakers and developers on the same page.

**Position B — graded scale, lesser status, or no label**: Three objections share one target, the single authoritative line. Yaniv Benhamou and Michel Reymond of the University of Geneva call the OSAID a "take it or leave it" approach and prefer several tiers of openness, as in the Linux Foundation's [[ModelOpennessFramework|Model Openness Framework]]. Radboud University's Andreas Liesenfeld and Mark Dingemanse ground a similar view in a survey, arguing that openness is composite and gradient, so an open/closed split loses information. Bradley M. Kühn of the Software Freedom Conservancy objects instead to the document's rank: the OSI should have issued "recommendations" or "guidelines," not a "definition" with authority equal to the OSD. He also rejects the urgency warrant. The OSI never sought trademark protection for "open source," so it cannot stop Mark Zuckerberg from calling Llama open source in any event, and its haste cost it independence: "Their celerity of response made OSI an easy target for regulatory capture." In June 2024, during drafting, OSS Capital founder Joseph Jacks went furthest and held that open source cannot be carried over to model weights at all. Of the three, Kühn's rank objection is the one still pressed in 2026: his call for repeal has since been joined by Red Hat counsel Richard Fontana.

**C — Mediation**: Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller of Open Future accept the binary OSAID as a strong standard for developers that makes corporate open-washing easy to identify ([[open-future-osaid-step-forward|their assessment]]). They still hold that a tiered standard would have been the easier answer, and they call for a shared community norm that supports a gradient of approaches beyond the definition's single line.

## Representative Evidence

- [[osaid-take-it-or-leave-it#Key Claims|Kluwer Copyright Blog analysis]] — Benhamou and Reymond (Kluwer Copyright Blog, March 2025) read the OSAID against the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]]. The OSAID requires the “complete source code used to train and run the system” but not the training data itself; save for that data, every component must grant the four freedoms, a test they find tracks the Act's Article 53(2) open source definition closely. They welcome it as a curb on industry capture, but they expect it to leave only a subset of AI projects compatible and conclude that a tiered notion "may be preferred" — an [analysis]-grade preference, hedged in their own wording.
- [[rethinking-open-source-generative-ai#Key Quotes|Liesenfeld and Dingemanse's ACM FAccT '24 paper]] — a peer-reviewed paper for the June 2024 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency that scores 40 text generators and 6 text-to-image generators on 14 openness dimensions. Its central finding is that today's systems "can only be classified into open or closed at the price of severe information loss," so "it is no longer feasible to maintain a simple open/closed binary." Its open-washing charge is narrower: it targets licence-only or weights-only tests, and the AI Act's licence-based exemption. Their proposed middle ground: "Between radical openness and homeopathic openness lies meaningful openness."
- [[sfc-osaid-erodes-open-source#Key Quotes|Software Freedom Conservancy post]] — Kühn (October 2024) targets the OSAID's rank as well as its content: "The humble act now is to admit that it was just too soon to publish a “definition”," and he asks that the text be republished as "current recommendations." He announced a single-issue run for the OSI Board to have the OSAID repealed.
- [[open-weights-not-open-source-label-dispute#Key Quotes|The Register column]] — a September 2026 column by Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols reports that the opposition outlasted the release. It carries Kühn and Fontana's call for repeal, which the column does not date: "The OSI acted too quickly to impose an overly ambitious policy compromise on the community," the definition "created a rift in the FOSS community," and it "shows no signs of having any positive policy influence" on practitioners, the community or regulators.
- [[mozilla-celebrates-osaid#Key Quotes|Mozilla endorsement post]] — [[Mozilla]] (Ayah Bdeir, Imo Udom, Nik Marda) endorses the definition as "an important step forward" and argues a binary definition gives developers, advocates, and regulators needed precision and helps fight open-washing. The post itself calls the binary "just one of several approaches to defining open source AI."
- [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition#Key Quotes|TechCrunch release report]] — at the release, Maffulli states the standard's enforcement model: "Our hope is that when someone tries to abuse the term, the AI community will say, 'We don't recognize this as open source,' and it gets corrected." Kyle Wiggers reports the OSI has no enforcement mechanism of its own but intends to flag models that fall short, and that a committee will monitor how the OSAID is applied and propose amendments.
- [[open-future-osaid-step-forward#Key Quotes|Open Future assessment]] — Tarkowski and Keller (November 2024) record the OSI's reason for the form: it "has opted for a “binary”, non-tiered standard, arguing that it is necessary to clarify which system is compliant, and which is not." Against that, they argue that "the issue would be easiest to solve with a tiered standard," pointing to the Model Openness Framework's three tiers.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

The three objections did not replace one another; they coexist, and each is tied to a different moment. Beside those three, a fourth objection, about scope, preceded them all. The March 2024 draft set "ethical, trustworthy, or responsible" AI aside in an "out of scope issues" section, and Maffulli told MIT Technology Review that such questions are "not our job." Zuzanna Warso of Open Future answered that "the idea that technology is neutral and that topics like ethics are 'out of scope'" is a myth, one she suspects is upheld to keep the open-source coalition from fracturing ([[tech-industry-open-source-ai-definition-problem|MIT Technology Review]]). Her challenge is to the neutrality the test claims rather than to its form. The graded objection followed: the FAccT paper made it in June 2024, before the final text, and Open Future (November 2024) and Benhamou and Reymond (March 2025) restated it against the released definition. The naming objection also belongs to drafting. In June 2024 Jacks said the name should not apply at all, since weights "are not software source code," [[StefanoMaffulli|Maffulli]] conceded that "the point is correct," and the OSI kept the term because "everyone was already using the term" ([[what-does-open-source-ai-mean|TechCrunch drafting-stage feature]]).

The rank objection is specific to the October 2024 release. Kühn wanted the text demoted to recommendations. Bruce Perens, author of the original OSD and an OSI co-founder, wanted no separate AI definition at all: the plain OSD "can be applied to AI," and the OSI should have released "a procedure on how to do that" ([[case-against-osaid|The New Stack's round-up]]). Both deny the OSAID the OSD's rank; only Kühn would keep its text as guidance. The rank objection is also the one with the latest restatement. A September 2026 Register column carries Kühn and Fontana's call for repeal and a later declaration by Perens that goes beyond rank: "It's unfortunate that the Open Source Initiative itself is now involved in Openwashing." The corpus holds no OSI reply to the repeal call. The column carries only the OSI's acknowledgement at the October 2024 release that the definition would continue to evolve, and critics say its central shortcomings remain unresolved ([[open-weights-not-open-source-label-dispute|The Register]]).

Each camp carries a limit it admits itself:

- **The definition camp expects its line to move.** Maffulli said in June 2024 that "We can't really expect this definition to last for 26 years like the Open Source Definition" ([[what-does-open-source-ai-mean|TechCrunch drafting-stage feature]]), and the OSI calls the release a "stable version," not a final one. Who applies the line to individual systems is left unresolved. The OSI's reference page lists which systems pass and which do not, yet calls those results "not certifications of any kind" and says the OSI "will not validate or review individual AI systems" ([[osi-open-source-ai-definition|OSI reference page]]); at the release it also said it would flag models that fall short. RedMonk's Stephen O'Grady names the cost of that ambiguity: the OSD succeeded because a licence either complies or does not, while the OSAID "requires explanation to be understood" ([[osi-readies-controversial-osaid|LWN.net]]).
- **The graded critics keep a cutoff.** Liesenfeld and Dingemanse allow that graded judgements can be used "to define an openness cutoff for regulatory purposes" ([[rethinking-open-source-generative-ai|the FAccT paper]]); their quarrel is with deciding on a single feature, not with a line as such. Their paper also predates the OSAID's final text and targets licence-focused definition efforts, while the final OSAID also requires code, parameters, and data information. Benhamou and Reymond hedge their own preference ("may be preferred") and expect that, with the OSAID and the AI Act taking similar approaches, the definition of open source in AI may well stay narrowly framed regardless.
- **The repeal camp doubts any line for now.** Kühn expects it to take "at least a decade" to find the bright line of acceptable behaviour, and he says he does not yet know whether open training sets are the only way to respect user rights. His own post notes that the OSI's by-laws let the sitting Board reject election results, which limits his chosen route to repeal ([[sfc-osaid-erodes-open-source|Conservancy post]]).

The steward has used a graded vocabulary below its line since the release. The October 2024 reference page already set [[OpenWeights|open weights]] models apart, sending readers to a dedicated page, and a September 2026 OSI blog post by Katie Steen-James restates the ladder: AI systems are "closed, open-weight, or Open Source," and open weights allow "some freedoms, but not all, and only to a certain degree" ([[osi-open-weights-good-open-source-better|OSI blog]]). That is a three-rung ladder with the OSAID as the only top rung: graded words below the line, a pass/fail test at the top.

## Interpretive Direction

On this evidence the camps appear closer on mechanics than on authority. The FAccT authors accept a cutoff drawn over graded evidence, a tiered scheme such as the Model Openness Framework is itself a set of cutoffs, and the OSI has always ranked what falls below its line. What stays contested is whether one steward's line should carry the name "open source" and the standing of the OSD. The two sides ground that authority in different evidence. The OSI grounds its line in the need for a common understanding among policymakers; Kühn grounds his objection in the OSD's own origin, published in 1999 at least twelve years after industry adoption of free software and developed chiefly by Debian. The evidence on both sides is [analysis]-grade argument, each point resting on attribution to a named claimant; the corpus holds no measurement of how far the positions actually diverge in practice, or of whether a binary or a graded test better curbs open-washing. Kühn and Fontana's claim that the definition has had no positive policy influence is an assertion, not a measurement, and so is the OSI's claim that a single line gives policymakers a common understanding. As of 2026-09-30 this corpus records no revision of the OSAID, no output from its amendment committee, no result of Kühn's board run, and no regulator adopting the [[ModelOpennessFramework|framework's tiers]].

Each side has a signal to watch, at least in the short term. If a single definition works, firms should drop the "open source" label for models that fail it; Maffulli reported that Google and Microsoft agreed to drop it, while [[Meta]] has not ([[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition|TechCrunch]]). If graded openness serves better, rules such as the [[EUAIAct|AI Act]]'s transparency template should move toward tiered measures. If the repeal camp's reading prevails, the OSI would republish the text as recommendations or change its board. For the wider landscape, see the [[open-source-ai-definition|Open-Source AI Definition overview]] and the synthesis on [[open-source-ai-every-camp-standard|what clears every camp's bar]].

# Residual Fragmentary Issues

## Opposing Positions

This page holds the source-to-source disagreements that fit no named theme. They share no single axis. Five disagreements sit here, and they fall into three groups by character: a forecast overtaken by events, one license review read in opposite ways, and two single-pair disputes that no other source joins.

**Forecast overtaken by events**: In April 2025 LWN reported that a Debian proposal to deny free-software status to AI models shipped without their [[TrainingData|training data]] looked likely to pass. Its later report records the proposal's withdrawal before any vote. The two reports do not oppose each other; the second supersedes the first's forecast.

**One review, opposite readings**: The [[LinuxFoundation|Linux Foundation]] presents its [[OpenMDW]] model license as clear and permissive. Reviewers on the [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] (OSI) license-review mailing list objected to specific clauses in its text. Former OSI executive director [[StefanoMaffulli|Stefano Maffulli]] attributes those objections to ideological bias. The two disagreements here are about the same review.

**Single-pair disputes**: A guide to the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]] reads the European Commission's guidelines as letting a free and open-source license carry proportionate safety restrictions. The OSI had argued, against earlier drafts of the EU Code of Practice, that use restrictions conflict with the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] ([[osi-eu-code-of-practice-open-source|OSI on the EU Code of Practice]]). Separately, the industry open letter *Open Weights and American AI Leadership*, hosted by Microsoft, says [[OpenWeights|open weights]] help organizations avoid lock-in ([[open-weights-american-ai-leadership|the letter]]). Chinmayi Sharma, an associate professor at Fordham Law School writing in Lawfare, argues they are not inherently sovereignty-enhancing.

## Representative Evidence

*Forecast overtaken by events*

- [[debian-ai-models-dfsg#Key Claims|LWN, "Debian debates AI models and the DFSG"]] — Joe Brockmeier reported on 2025-04-25 that Mo Zhou's General Resolution already had enough sponsors, and that "if the discussion reflects the overall mood of Debian developers, the GR would be likely to pass."
- [[debian-ai-gr-withdrawn#Key Claims|LWN, "Debian AI General Resolution withdrawn"]] — Brockmeier then reports that Zhou withdrew the proposal on May 8, 2025, saying the community was unprepared to vote. Objections had grown that trained components of spam filters, OCR tools and text-to-speech packages would become non-free.

*One review, opposite readings*

- [[openmdw-1-1-nvidia-adoption#Key Quotes|Linux Foundation, OpenMDW-1.1 release]] — the Linux Foundation's own press release, from 2026-05-28, quotes CEO Jim Zemlin: NVIDIA's adoption helps establish "a clear and permissive framework for sharing AI models at global scale." No outside assessment of the license appears in it.
- [[lwn-openmdw-license-review#Key Quotes|LWN, "Considering the OpenMDW license"]] — Pamela Chestek calls the duty to clear third-party rights "an impossibility generally," and Richard Fontana calls the termination clause too broad. Simon Phipps writes that "a license revoking Freedom 0 upon a copyright claim cannot assure software freedom."
- [[open-weights-not-open-source-label-dispute#Key Quotes|The Register, "Open weights are not open source"]] — Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols quotes Maffulli: "I continue getting the impression that the OpenMDW review is tainted by an ideological bias." The column itself calls the OpenMDW approach reasonable and ends without a verdict.

*Single-pair disputes*

- [[eu-ai-act-gpai-guide-open-source-developers#Key Claims|Osborne et al., EU AI Act guide for open-source developers]] — the authors read paragraphs 78 and 83 of the Commission's guidelines as excluding research-only and acceptable-use licenses. They read paragraph 84 as allowing "specific, proportionate, and safety-oriented usage restrictions" where the licensor sees a significant risk.
- [[open-weight-diplomacy-digital-silk-road#Key Claims|Sharma, "Open-Weight Diplomacy"]] — Sharma grants that a weight file can be copied and "kept forever," unlike a Huawei switch. She argues that countries adopting Chinese models grow dependent "because of the next release they don't have."

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

Each group stays out of the named themes for its own reason. The Debian pair touches the question of the [[open-training-data-requirement|OSAID Definition vs Open Training-Data Requirement]] theme, but the two reports agree on the substance. What changed between them is the event, so the pair is a time-shifted update rather than an opposition. The withdrawal did not settle the question: Zhou said he would need a few months before returning to it ([[debian-ai-gr-withdrawn|LWN]]). As of 2026-09, no later report is in these sources.

The OpenMDW pair is the only group with a shared axis. Both disagreements concern whether a license written for whole model distributions meets the OSI's standard. The Linux Foundation's side is voiced mainly through its own release and Mike Dolan's replies on the list, where he defends the termination clause as symmetry. A model publisher's exposure runs under copyright, he argues, so a patent-only provision "would replicate Apache-2.0's form while abandoning its function." Chestek answered that the license is not symmetric: the model's producer may know whether it copied protected material, while the recipient gives up its copyright claims without knowing whether they were infringed ([[lwn-openmdw-license-review|LWN]]).

Maffulli's charge moves the question from the license text to the reviewers. The dispute is over how to read the objections: LWN reports them as clause-level defects, while Maffulli reads them as motive. His charge carries weight as the view of the executive who led the OSI's own definition, yet it comes from a single quote ([[open-weights-not-open-source-label-dispute|The Register]]).

The EU pair is narrower than its wording suggests. The OSI's post, from March 2025, objected to Code of Practice drafts that *mandated* acceptable use policies: under those drafts, it wrote, "developers would have to choose between complying with the Code of Practice or being Open Source." It reports that the third draft made them optional ([[osi-eu-code-of-practice-open-source|OSI]]). The guide, from August 2025, reads a different instrument, the Commission's guidelines, as *permitting* some restrictions ([[eu-ai-act-gpai-guide-open-source-developers|the guide]]). The two meet only at the level of principle: whether any use restriction can sit inside an open-source license. The same principle drives the [[llama-open-source-label|vendor-label theme]], but there the subject is a vendor's claim, not a regulator's definition. The OSI's answer to paragraph 84 itself is not in these sources.

Sharma's argument sits beside the [[open-weights-safety-tradeoff|safety theme]] without joining it. That theme asks whether open release helps defenders or attackers; Sharma sets misuse risk aside as real but not the largest stake. Her subject is dependency through compute, cloud and the next release ([[open-weight-diplomacy-digital-silk-road|Sharma]]). The pairing with the open weights letter rests on a match of signatories. Sharma describes an industry letter, without giving its title, signed by Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta and over 230 other companies and organizations. The Microsoft-hosted letter lists the same three companies and reports more than 270 signatories as of 2026-08-03. Its case is that organizations "want to know that they will not become locked into a single provider or lose the knowledge and capabilities they build over time." The letter's signatories give no reply to her in these sources ([[open-weights-american-ai-leadership|the letter]]).

The pairs differ in how far their disagreement has gone. Only the EU pair gives opposite answers to one question, and even there the two sides read different instruments. The OpenMDW objections are specific and quoted, but their motive is contested. The Sharma pair is a claim that has drawn no reply, and the Debian pair records an update.

## Interpretive Direction

On current evidence, the OpenMDW pair looks like the nearest candidate for promotion out of this bucket. LWN reports that the OSI does not appear ready to approve the license in its current form, and that the changes needed have not been specified. An OSI decision, a revised license, or a Linux Foundation answer to the reviewers would each add claims on the same axis. The EU guide's authors, who include Linux Foundation researchers writing in a personal capacity, also judge that OpenMDW likely qualifies as a free and open-source license under the Act. That opinion predates the dispute: the guide appeared on 2025-08-04, before OpenMDW-1.1 (2026-05-28) and LWN's report on the OSI review (2026-08), and it tests the license against the Act's own definition of a free and open-source license, not the Open Source Definition. Restated against the OSI's standard, it could form a third side. If such claims accumulate to theme size, a theme on model-specific licenses against the Open Source Definition could absorb both the OpenMDW pair and the EU pair, since both turn on what an open license may restrict.

The other items currently point elsewhere. If Debian revives the resolution, the new reports belong in the training-data theme, and the withdrawal becomes that theme's history. Sharma's dependency argument would need answers from other sources before it could stand as an axis. Adjacent Lawfare and Stanford HAI pieces cover the same Chinese releases ([[knives-out-open-weight-ai-models|Lawfare, "Knives Are Out"]]; [[beyond-deepseek-china-open-weight-ecosystem|Stanford HAI, "Beyond DeepSeek"]]), and they are the likely starting points. The landscape these fragments sit in is surveyed in the [[open-source-ai-definition|Open-Source AI Definition]], [[open-weights|Open Weights]] and [[open-model-governance|Open-Model Governance]] cluster overviews. The [[distillation-free-riding-dispute|distillation theme]] and the [[osaid-form-and-legitimacy|definition-legitimacy theme]] complete the set of named themes.
