# Meta's 'open source' label for Llama vs the OSI and critics' rejection

## Opposing Positions

[[Meta]] has called each generation of its Llama models "open source," from Llama 2 in July 2023 to Llama 3.1 a year later. The [[OpenSourceInitiative]] (OSI) and a line of independent researchers reject the label. They hold that Llama's license limits who may use the model and for what, and that the release withholds training code and data. The clash is over who sets the meaning of the term for AI models: the company that ships them, or the steward of the software definition.

**Position A** (Meta: the label describes a free release that ships its weights): Meta's own announcements carry this side in the first person. The [[llama-2-meta-microsoft|Llama 2 announcement]] calls the model "the next generation of our open source large language model," free for research and commercial use, with [[OpenWeights|weights]] and starting code. Mark Zuckerberg's [[open-source-ai-path-forward|2024 letter]] calls Llama 3.1 405B "the first frontier-level open source AI model" and compares Llama's path to Linux overtaking closed Unix. When the OSI published its definition, a Meta spokesperson said Meta disagrees with it: "There is no single open source AI definition." The spokesperson also defended the license and its acceptable use policy as guardrails against harmful deployments. On the withheld components, Meta says it is taking a "cautious approach" to sharing model details, including training data, while regulations such as California's training transparency law evolve. Against the OSI's authority, the spokesperson pointed to other codifications: the Linux Foundation's suggested definitions, the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation]]'s criteria for free machine learning applications, and proposals from other researchers. Within this corpus, Meta never answers the OSI's clause-by-clause reading of the license. Its defense is that software-era definitions do not fit AI, that its restrictions serve [[AISafety|safety]], and that disclosure waits on regulation.

**Position B** (OSI and critics: the license and the withheld components decide): The OSI's [[osi-meta-llama-2-license-not-open-source|2023 post]] by [[StefanoMaffulli]] rules the Llama 2 license out under [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] (OSD) points 5 and 6, which bar discrimination against persons, groups, or fields of endeavor. It names two offending terms: "restrictions on commercial use for some users (paragraph 2)" and the purpose bans in the Acceptable Use Policy. In 2025 the OSI said Llama 3.x "is still not Open Source by any stretch of the imagination." Researchers reach the same verdict on other grounds. They find Llama "at best open weights," closed on training code, data, and documentation.

## Representative Evidence

- [[osi-meta-llama-2-license-not-open-source#Key Quotes|OSI, July 2023]] — the historical origin of the dispute. Maffulli writes that "Meta is confusing 'open source' with 'resources available to some users under some conditions,'" and says the OSI asked Meta to correct the statement. He also concedes the other side's motive: the OSI "does not question Meta's desire to limit the use of Llama for competitive purposes."
- [[llama-2-meta-microsoft#Key Claims|Meta, Llama 2 release]] — Meta's [fact]-grade account of what it shipped: model weights and starting code for pretrained and [[FineTuning|fine-tuned]] versions, free of charge, paired with an Acceptable Use Policy that prohibits certain use cases. The post reproduces no license terms, so the label rests on the release rather than on the grant.
- [[llama-3-1-community-license#Key Quotes|Llama 3.1 Community License]] — the [[ModelLicensing|license]] text behind the later label. It grants a royalty-free right to use, modify, and redistribute. It also requires "Llama" at the start of any derived model's name, binds all use to the Acceptable Use Policy, and withholds the grant from licensees above 700 million monthly active users unless Meta agrees.
- [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition#Key Quotes|TechCrunch on the OSI definition, October 2024]] — the only source where Meta answers the definition. Its spokesperson says "we, like others across the industry, disagree with their new definition," despite Meta's part in drafting it, and cites evolving regulation for its caution over training-data details. Maffulli says Google and Microsoft dropped the term for models that are not fully open after talks with the OSI, but Meta has not.
- [[rethinking-open-source-generative-ai#Key Claims|Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, FAccT '24]] — a peer-reviewed survey, presented at the ACM FAccT fairness and accountability conference, of 40 text generators and 6 text-to-image systems scored on 14 openness dimensions. It concludes that "Meta's Llama is at best open weights" and that Llama 3 "is no different from Llama 2 in terms of openness." Its grounds are components, not license: no training code, a one-line description of the base data, and weights behind a consent form. The authors also warn that the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]]'s exemption for models under a free and open licence makes the label worth claiming, since it lifts documentation duties.
- [[what-does-open-source-ai-mean#Key Quotes|TechCrunch, June 2024]] — Maffulli claims consensus against the label: "Everyone else that is involved in the conversation is perfectly agreeing that Llama itself cannot be considered open source," adding that people at Meta "know that it's a little bit of a stretch." The attribution is his alone; no Meta employee is quoted.
- [[osi-meta-llama-license-still-not-open-source#Key Claims|OSI, February 2025]] — Jordan Maris finds the Llama 3.x licenses still fail OSD points 5 and 6. He endorses the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation]]'s view that they fail freedom 0, and reports that newer versions exclude persons in the European Union. He also argues the OSI's AI definition is not needed to reach this verdict.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

**The critics agree on the verdict but not on the test.** The OSI's two blog posts make a license case. Maris says the [[OpenSourceAI|Open Source AI Definition]] (OSAID) is not needed to judge Llama, because the old software freedoms already fail. The FAccT researchers make a component case instead. They warn that basing openness on a single measure, such as the mere availability of weights or an open licence, is one of the most reductive methods and an easy route to [[OpenWashing]], and they name the OSI's own consultation as too focused on licensing. The split matters for any exit. Meta could in principle meet the OSI's OSD objection by rewriting the license, though the OSI's OSAID validation would still ask for information on the data. It could not meet the researchers' objection without also releasing training code and [[TrainingData]] detail, the very disclosure on which Meta says it is taking a "cautious approach" while regulation evolves. Its appeal to other codifications does not escape the license case either: the Free Software Foundation, whose criteria the spokesperson cited, found the Llama 3.1 license fails freedom 0, and the OSI's February 2025 post endorses that finding. Open Future's Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller [[open-future-osaid-step-forward|combine both grounds]], rejecting the label for "non-OSI compliant licenses" and for carrying no information on the training data.

**Meta's wording has moved, but not away from the label.** Paul Sawers reports that with Llama 3 in April 2024 Meta shifted toward "openly available" and "openly accessible," while still saying "open source" in places. The FAccT paper quotes the Llama 3 blog calling it "open source" all the same. By July 2024 the Zuckerberg letter uses the term outright, and the letter is filed here as contradicting the OSI's 2023 request to correct it. So the retreat Sawers saw proved partial and short, at least in the documents this corpus holds.

**The steward has no lever, and Meta is among its funders.** The OSI has no enforcement mechanism; it can only flag models that fall short. Bradley M. Kühn of the Software Freedom Conservancy argues that because the OSI never secured a trademark for "open source," it [[sfc-osaid-erodes-open-source|cannot stop Zuckerberg]] from using the term for Llama. Both TechCrunch pieces also report that [[Meta]] is among the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]]'s corporate funders. Sawers raises the possible conflict of interest, and [[StefanoMaffulli|Maffulli]] answers that the OSI "could say goodbye to Meta's money anytime."

**Llama became an argument inside the definition fight.** Open Future reads Meta's public disagreement as evidence that the OSAID already pressures firms not to call partly open models "open source." The OSI, for its part, accuses unnamed critics of the definition of tying their complaints to Meta's use of the term, and says the dispute predates the OSAID entirely. One qualifier limits the OSI's side: its [[osi-open-source-ai-definition|validation list]], which placed Llama 2 among systems that do not pass, is framed on the OSI's own page as part of the definitional process and "not certifications of any kind."

## Interpretive Direction

On the evidence in this corpus, the dispute is not converging. Meta's side rests on first-person announcements and one spokesperson statement; the critics' side rests on a published clause-by-clause reading, a scored survey, and the OSI's repeated demands. That asymmetry is one of form as much as weight. Meta disputes the definition's authority rather than the reading of its own license, so the two sides do not meet on a shared test. Read the opposition as a labeling dispute between named parties, not as a measured factual conflict, and weigh each claim by who makes it and on what evidence.

In the short term, four points are worth monitoring. First, whether Meta relicenses a later Llama under terms that clear OSD points 5 and 6; the corpus currently records no Meta reply to the OSI's 2025 post. Second, whether the OSI's flag-without-enforcement approach moves Meta the way Maffulli says it moved Google and Microsoft. Third, the test of Meta's own position: whether the alternative codifications it points to, or the EU AI Act's open-source definition, which Open Future notes does not require training data to be shared, come to accept Llama-style releases as open, and whether the "others across the industry" Meta invokes keep using the term. Fourth, whether the AI Act's exemption turns a branding dispute into a compliance one. The license terms themselves are tracked under [[ModelLicensing]]. The wider landscape sits in the [[open-source-ai-definition|Open-Source AI Definition]] and [[open-weights|Open Weights]] overviews, which weigh whether Llama is better called an open-weights release.

# OSAID Definition vs Open Training-Data Requirement

## Opposing Positions

The [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] (OSI) released its Open Source AI Definition (OSAID) in October 2024. The definition lets a system count as open source without releasing its [[TrainingData|training data]], provided it discloses detailed "data information" about that data. The dispute turns on one technical question: is training data part of the "preferred form for making modifications" — the test the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] applies to source code — or a useful extra? One camp answers that data is a benefit and weights plus code are what people modify. The other answers that data is the source of a model, so a definition that makes it optional is not open source. The wider landscape of the definition is surveyed in [[open-source-ai-definition|the Open-Source AI Definition overview]].

**Position A — training data as a benefit**: The OSI stated in its 0.0.9 draft that training data "is not part of the preferred form" because its insights "have already been learned." Its FAQ defends the choice as what lets open source AI exist where data cannot legally be shared, such as medical AI. OSI executive director [[StefanoMaffulli|Stefano Maffulli]] rejects the charge that this lowers the bar. He attributes the preferred-form finding to the definition's endorsers and to Carnegie Mellon University's comment, not to himself or the OSI board, and argues that neither the more-components approach nor the weights-and-architecture approach is optimal. Red Hat CTO Chris Wright reaches the same conclusion from a vendor's side: most community improvement happens by modifying weights or through [[FineTuning|fine-tuning]], and data's link to the trained weights is "tenuous and indirect." Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller of Open Future add a supply argument — open resources lack the volume to train large foundation models, so nearly every open effort depends on web-scraped data.

**Position B — training data as a requirement**: The [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|Free Software Foundation]] (FSF) holds that a machine-learning application is not free unless its training data and processing scripts respect the four freedoms. AWS open source strategists Tom Callaway and julia ferraioli press the same line. Callaway calls a data-optional definition a "loophole" that devalues "open source" in every other context, and ferraioli argues that without data users can only use a system and build on it by fine-tuning. OSI co-founder Bruce Perens holds that the training data is the source code, so the original Open Source Definition could cover it, and judges the OSAID "less than Open Source." A Debian General Resolution proposed by Mo Zhou would carry the position into distribution policy, beyond the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]]'s own forum. It would declare models released without their original training data non-free under the Debian Free Software Guidelines (DFSG).

**Position C — keep the floor, change the name**: Tarkowski and Keller accept the data-information floor as the right call for developers. They argue the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]] should have named it after [[OpenWeights|open weights]] and reserved "open source" for systems with open data. They frame the choice as "a stronger but narrower standard" against "a weaker but broader" one, and say a tiered scheme like the [[ModelOpennessFramework|Model Openness Framework]] would have resolved it.

## Representative Evidence

- [[osi-readies-controversial-osaid#Key Quotes|LWN's pre-vote survey]] (2024-10-25, the oldest source on this axis) — Joe Brockmeier sets the OSI's own voice beside its critics: the 0.0.9 announcement calls data "a benefit, not a requirement," Maffulli answers "We have not compromised on our principles," and the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|FSF]] states "we cannot say a ML application 'is free' unless all its training data" respects the four freedoms.
- [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition#Key Quotes|TechCrunch on the 1.0 release]] — Kyle Wiggers reports the release requirement that a model be documented well enough to be "substantially" recreated, and quotes Lightning AI CTO Luca Antiga: "By neglecting to deal with licensing of training data, the OSI is leaving a gaping hole."
- [[open-future-osaid-step-forward#Key Quotes|Open Future's assessment]] — Tarkowski and Keller call training datasets "the primary component that can be understood as the source of AI systems," yet judge the absence of an open-data requirement "the correct choice" from developers' perspective.
- [[red-hat-open-source-ai-point-of-view#Key Quotes|Red Hat's point of view]] — Chris Wright sets Red Hat's minimum threshold as "open source-licensed model weights combined with open source software components" and writes that "Training data alone does not fit this role."
- [[case-against-osaid#Key Quotes|The New Stack's round-up]] — David Cassel collects the critics and reports Perens's view that the training data is the source code. Against the OSI FAQ's forecast that an open-data requirement would leave open source AI a "tiny" niche, he lists open-data releases: a 2-trillion-token dataset from Pleias, LLMs with open training data from Ai2, and "fully open 1 billion parameter language models" from AMD.
- [[debian-ai-models-dfsg#Key Quotes|LWN on the Debian resolution]] (2025-04-25) — Mo Zhou's text holds that models "without original training data or program" are not DFSG-compliant, and Gunnar Wolf argues Debian "cannot magically extend DFSG-freeness to a binary we have no way to recreate."

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

Both camps concede ground to the other, and those concessions shape the dispute more than the headline positions do. All three voices that raise medical data — the OSI FAQ, Callaway and the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|FSF]] — concede it as the case where withholding training data is defensible; they differ on the label. The FAQ keeps such systems inside open source, the FSF calls them nonfree but possibly "ethically excusable" to use, and Callaway would accept "less Open Source AI systems at first" rather than compromise. Callaway also grants the other reasons to withhold data — its monetary value, redistribution bans, copyright risk. On the other side, Red Hat calls its weights-first threshold "a starting point of open source AI, not the final destination." [[Mozilla]], an endorser, acknowledges in [[mozilla-celebrates-osaid|its endorsement post]] that some disagree with the training-data treatment and that the definition will need refinement. Both camps also claim to fight [[OpenWashing|open-washing]]. The canonical [[osi-open-source-ai-definition|OSAID text]] names combating it as the definition's motivation, while Open Future reports that Open Data advocates see the missing data requirement as itself "a form of open washing."

The strict camp is not one position either. "Open data" can mean access to the dataset, a distributable license for it, or reproducible training from it, and different critics stress different ones. Antiga's objection is about [[ModelLicensing|licensing]] of the data, not disclosure. In Debian, Thorsten Glaser's counter-proposal adds that models be trained only on "legally obtained and used works" and be rebuilt reproducibly — a stance he himself calls "a hard anti-AI stance (with select exceptions)." Zhou's proposal also lists as a downside that almost no useful AI models could enter Debian's main archive, which is the practical cost the A camp predicts.

The dispute has also moved venues over time. It began inside the OSI's co-design process: the 0.0.9 draft of August 2024 already named training data "one of the most hotly debated parts." Post-release criticism followed in late 2024. In February 2025 Red Hat set out its own minimum threshold, which it says is not a formal definition. In April 2025 the question reached Debian's governance. Along the way the corpus records an industry trend toward less data transparency. Open Future ties it to competition and copyright liability, and [[Meta]] told TechCrunch it takes a "cautious approach" to sharing model details, including details about its training data. The generational reading runs through the history of the term. Perens holds that the inherited definition, with data treated as source code, applies to AI unchanged. Maffulli and RedMonk's Stephen O'Grady, from opposite conclusions, agree it cannot simply be translated to AI.

A note on the evidence grade: nearly every claim here is `[analysis]`-grade argument by a named claimant, with `[fact]`-grade material limited to what each definition or proposal requires. The corpus holds no measurement of how often data access actually changes what modifiers can do, so each camp's central premise rests on attribution to practitioners rather than on data. Only one source rebuts the other side head-on: Red Hat's point of view rejects the premise, reported in [[case-against-osaid|the round-up of critics]] from Perens, that training data is the equivalent of a model's source code.

## Interpretive Direction

In the short term, what [[OpenSourceAI|open source AI]] requires of data looks set to be settled by different institutions, not within one. The OSAID's data-information floor stands as the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]]'s reference, while the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|FSF]]'s pending criteria and the Debian resolution would set a data-required bar for free software. Open Future's "stronger but narrower" against "weaker but broader" framing describes the trade-off without choosing a winner, and the evidence here does not settle which side of it the community will take. The open-data releases in the round-up answer the objection, which one observer calls central, that there is "no good example of an open data LLM." At 1 billion parameters, though, AMD's models do not reach the scale objections the other side actually makes: Open Future's, that open resources are too thin to train large foundation models, and the OSI FAQ's, that a data requirement would leave a "tiny" niche. Nor do the releases show data should be mandatory, and a companion analysis, [[open-source-ai-every-camp-standard|what clears every camp's bar]], finds no release documented as meeting both standards.

Points to monitor, one set per camp. On the data-required side, watch whether the Debian resolution passed — the corpus records Brockmeier's forecast that it was likely to, but no vote result as of 2026-09-24 — and whether the FSF publishes its free machine-learning criteria or the OSI's amendment committee revisits the data clause. On the data-information side, watch whether the EU AI Act's training-data transparency template takes the OSAID floor as its reference point, a role Open Future argues the OSAID can play for the template blueprint it drafted with Mozilla and several open source AI developers. The newest source on this axis dates from April 2025, so any of these may already have moved outside the corpus. The parallel dispute over the OSAID's binary form and its board-level legitimacy is followed in [[osaid-form-and-legitimacy|the binary-definition theme]].

# Openness as a safety mechanism vs open weights as uniquely hard to govern

## Opposing Positions

Both sides in these sources accept that released model weights leave the developer's control; they split over what that fact calls for. Advocates of open release argue that broad scrutiny is itself a safety mechanism and that closed models carry risks of their own, so the answer is wider access. Critics argue that [[OpenWeights|open weights]] are uniquely hard to govern, because safeguards can be stripped from a downloaded model and a release cannot be recalled, so the answer is restraint before release. The dispute sits inside the wider [[AISafety|AI safety]] debate.

**Position A (openness as a safety mechanism)**: The open weights letter, dated 2026-07-24, hosted by Microsoft and signed by more than 270 companies and organizations including [[Meta]] and [[Mozilla]], argues that "openness may be one of the most important paths to AI safety and security." Its signatories hold that a broad community can examine model behavior and find vulnerabilities, and that closed models can fail in ways outsiders cannot detect. They also argue that cybersecurity defenders need access to models comparable to those attackers use. An undated joint statement hosted by Mozilla puts it more strongly: "When it comes to AI safety and security, openness is an antidote, not a poison." Meta has made the argument since the Llama 2 release, when it said community stress testing makes open models safer, and [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]] CEO Clem Delangue restated the defensive version in 2026-08.

**Position B (open weights as uniquely hard to govern)**: David Evan Harris, a UC Berkeley scholar and former Meta employee writing in IEEE Spectrum in 2024-01, argues that "unsecured AI poses an enormous risk that we are not yet able to contain." He points to "Llama 2 Uncensored," a derivative with its safety features removed, and argues that jailbreaks of secured systems can be patched while a stripped release cannot. He proposes pausing all new unsecured releases until safety features cannot easily be removed. [[Anthropic]] CEO Dario Amodei says Anthropic has never advocated a ban on open-weights models as a category, but he rejects the open weights letter's claims that open weights necessarily make safeguards easier or help defenders more than attackers: "It seems at least as likely to me that the opposite will be true." Henry Papadatos of the evaluation group SaferAI calls the defensive benefit often overstated, after SaferAI found that Z.ai's open-weight model GLM-5.2 refused none of the offensive cyber or biology tasks it was given.

**Position C (measure marginal risk before deciding)**: A third line treats the question as open rather than settled. Sayash Kapoor and 24 co-authors find current research insufficient to characterize the marginal misuse risk of open models against pre-existing technologies. The U.S. National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) recommends monitoring risks rather than restricting weights immediately. Amodei's remedy also belongs here: mandatory pre-release testing of all capable models, open and closed, on the view that "Whether open models do or don't pose an increased risk, and whether that risk can be mitigated, is something that should emerge from testing, rather than be decided in advance." Papadatos adds a technical remedy that would apply even to open release: pre-training data filtering, which strips offensive material from the training set before the model is trained. TechCrunch reports research suggesting it can reduce hazardous biological knowledge, but that it is much less practical for cybersecurity.

## Representative Evidence

- [[open-weights-american-ai-leadership#Key Quotes|Open Weights and American AI Leadership]] — the open weights letter's signatories, who include [[Meta]], [[Mozilla]], [[OpenAI]], and [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]], write that "Relying solely on closed models is not inherently safe." The same letter concedes: "To be sure, open weights carry real and distinct risks. Once released, the weights are beyond the original developer's control, and modified versions are difficult to trace or reverse." It still asks policymakers not to prohibit them.
- [[joint-statement-ai-safety-openness#Key Quotes|Joint Statement on AI Safety and Openness]] — signatories listed with Mozilla, Meta, [[MistralAI|Mistral AI]], and the [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] call tight proprietary control of foundation models "naive at best, dangerous at worst." They acknowledge that openly available models can be abused by malicious actors, and the page links 1821 signatures.
- [[open-source-ai-uniquely-dangerous#Key Quotes|Harris, "Open-Source AI Is Uniquely Dangerous"]] — "Once someone releases an 'uncensored' version of an unsecured AI system, the original maker of the system is largely powerless to do anything about it." His harms (misinformation, scams, chemical and biological weapons) are carried as [analysis] and [forecast] claims; the piece records no measured incident.
- [[open-weight-models-frontier-safety-gap#Key Quotes|TechCrunch on the SaferAI evaluation]] — SaferAI reports that GLM-5.2 trails OpenAI's GPT-5.5 and Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.7 by only a few months on cyber and biology capability, and that Z.ai published no safety framework or risk assessment. The same article reports that Hugging Face relied on the model to defend itself during a recent breach. Papadatos answers that the benefit does not justify open-sourcing dangerous capabilities: "The main point in my mind is that we shouldn't just accept that dangerous capabilities are easily accessible by anyone anywhere."
- [[anthropic-position-open-weights-models#Key Quotes|Amodei, "Our position on open-weights models"]] — Amodei agrees with the open weights letter on access, competition, and handling [[Distillation|distillation]] through targeted legal frameworks. He argues that open-weights models potentially carry higher risk than closed ones, because guardrails and usage monitoring are hard to apply and released weights cannot be withdrawn. He expects biology in particular to favor attackers.
- [[senators-question-meta-llama-leak#Key Quotes|VentureBeat on the LLaMA leak letter]] — the earliest exchange in these sources, from 2023-06. In the senators' letter, Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley questioned Meta's "unrestrained and permissive" distribution of LLaMA over spam, fraud, and malware. Stanford's Christopher Manning answered, "I do not consider their release an unacceptable risk," and Vipul Ved Prakash, CEO of Together, whose RedPajama project replicated the LLaMA dataset, called the senators' letter "full of typical straw-man concerns."
- [[societal-impact-open-foundation-models#Key Claims|Kapoor et al., "On the Societal Impact of Open Foundation Models"]] — the paper proposes a framework for the marginal risk of misuse and finds that, for cyberattacks and bioweapons among other vectors, current research cannot yet characterize that risk. The source page is built from the arXiv abstract only.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

The object of concern moved over three years. In the 2023 exchange the worry was mid-sized models used for spam and fraud, and defenders could answer, as Manning did, that LLaMA was smaller than the largest closed models and that comparable open models already existed. By 2026 the worry had become near-frontier capability in cyber and biology, and SaferAI's evaluation supplied the first measured result in these sources. The defensive case changed shape in parallel. [[llama-2-meta-microsoft|Meta's Llama 2 announcement]] framed safety as community stress testing, and [[open-source-ai-path-forward|Mark Zuckerberg's 2024 letter]] added that wide deployment lets larger actors check smaller bad actors. The open weights letter recast this as a claim that defenders need parity with attackers.

Most pairings in this theme are reconstructed across sources rather than exchanged. Only Amodei answers the open weights letter itself, and TechCrunch sets Papadatos's reply against Delangue's post. The other pairings set 2023 replies to the senators against Harris's essay, written seven months later. The spam concern belongs to the senators' letter; Harris names misinformation and scams instead. Several voices are also missing. Z.ai did not answer questions about its pre-release testing, [[Meta]] declined to comment on the senators' letter, and the IEEE Spectrum rebuttal paired with Harris's piece is not among the sources.

Each side concedes a limit of its own. Beyond the open weights letter's concession quoted above, the joint statement grants abuse by malicious actors, so neither claims that openness removes misuse risk, and their concrete defensive evidence is one company's reported use of one model, plus its CEO's social media post. Harris grants the [[OpenSourceAI|open-source]] movement a role against gatekeeping and writes that "nearly all of the regulations that have been publicly discussed apply to secured AI systems as well," which narrows what is unique to the loss of recall and control. SaferAI ran its test through Z.ai's public API, so its result measures the safeguards Z.ai shipped, not what happens once someone strips them from downloaded [[OpenWeights|weights]].

Motive is contested too. Adam Thierer of the R Street Institute suggested in 2023 that proprietary competitors see open source as "a form of competition that they would rather just see go away in some cases." Amodei answers that charge in the first person: some have accused Anthropic of wanting to ban open-weights models to protect its business, and he writes that barring US businesses from using such models would do nothing about the risk: "It would protect US AI companies from competition, but that has never been my goal." Harris turns the charge around, warning that makers of unsecured AI may profit by "pushing the risks posed by their products onto all of us." [[OpenAI]] appears on both sides of the record, since Harris cites its 2019 decision that releasing GPT weights was too dangerous and the open weights letter lists it as a signatory.

## Interpretive Direction

On these sources, the dispute turns less on the fact of lost control, which both sides grant, than on whether access helps defenders or attackers more. That question has so far been argued more than measured. Nearly every claim on either side carries an [analysis] or [forecast] grade, and the [fact]-grade material is thin: one SaferAI evaluation, the "Llama 2 Uncensored" derivative Harris documents, the open weights letter's own concession, and one reported defensive use. In the short term, the policy recommendations in these sources lean toward Position C rather than a ban. [[ntia-open-model-weights-report|NTIA]] recommends monitoring before restricting, and [[Anthropic]]'s Amodei proposes testing open and closed models alike before release.

The monitoring points are symmetric. If Position A holds, documented cases should appear in which defenders using open models stop attacks, or outside scrutiny finds and fixes flaws that a closed developer missed. If Position B holds, documented misuse should appear that traces to stripped or [[FineTuning|fine-tuned]] open weights, the kind Harris predicts but these sources, as of 2026-09, do not record. For Position C, the test is whether mandatory pre-release testing is adopted and whether it reaches developers such as Z.ai, as Amodei hopes a global regime might. A narrower technical signal is whether the pre-training filtering of [[TrainingData|training data]] that Papadatos proposes can be made to work for cyber capability, the domain where Position C's sources find it least practical. The wider release landscape is mapped in the [[open-weights|Open Weights overview]], and the concept-level summary sits in [[AISafety|AI safety]].

# A single binary definition vs graded openness or repeal

## Opposing Positions

The [[OpenSourceInitiative]] (OSI) published its Open Source AI Definition (OSAID) as a single pass/fail test: a system either grants the four freedoms over every required component, or it is not [[OpenSourceAI|open source AI]]. This dispute is not about which components the test demands — that question belongs to the [[open-training-data-requirement|training-data dispute]]. It is about the form and the standing of the test itself. Defenders want one bright line that carries the weight of the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] (OSD); critics want a graded scale, a lesser status such as "recommendations," or no "open source AI" label at all.

**Position A — one pass/fail definition**: The OSI chose a single, non-tiered standard so that it is clear which systems comply and which do not. That rationale reaches this corpus through Open Future's account of the decision; the OSI's own reference page frames the need as a "common understanding to educate policy makers" and a way to combat [[OpenWashing]]. [[Mozilla]] endorses the result because a binary definition "akin to the existing definition" offers "precision for developers, advocates and regulators." [[StefanoMaffulli]], speaking for the OSI at the release, supplies the urgency warrant: "Regulators are already watching the space," and a consensus definition puts policymakers and developers on the same page.

**Position B — graded scale, lesser status, or no label**: Three objections share one target, the single authoritative line. Yaniv Benhamou and Michel Reymond of the University of Geneva call the OSAID a "take it or leave it" approach and prefer several tiers of openness, as in the Linux Foundation's [[ModelOpennessFramework|Model Openness Framework]]. Radboud University's Andreas Liesenfeld and Mark Dingemanse ground a similar view in a survey, arguing that openness is composite and gradient, so an open/closed split loses information. Bradley M. Kühn of the Software Freedom Conservancy objects instead to the document's rank: the OSI should have issued "recommendations" or "guidelines," not a "definition" with authority equal to the OSD. He also rejects the urgency warrant. The OSI never sought trademark protection for "open source," so it cannot stop Mark Zuckerberg from calling Llama open source in any event, and its haste cost it independence: "Their celerity of response made OSI an easy target for regulatory capture." OSS Capital founder Joseph Jacks goes furthest and holds that open source cannot be carried over to model weights at all.

**C — Mediation**: Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller of Open Future accept the binary OSAID as a strong standard for developers that makes corporate open-washing easy to identify ([[open-future-osaid-step-forward|their assessment]]). They still hold that a tiered standard would have been the easier answer, and they call for a shared community norm that supports a gradient of approaches beyond the definition's single line.

## Representative Evidence

- [[osaid-take-it-or-leave-it#Key Claims|Kluwer Copyright Blog analysis]] — Benhamou and Reymond (Kluwer Copyright Blog, March 2025) read the OSAID against the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]]. The OSAID requires the “complete source code used to train and run the system” but not the training data itself; save for that data, every component must grant the four freedoms, a test they find tracks the Act's Article 53(2) open source definition closely. They welcome it as a curb on industry capture, but they expect it to leave only a subset of AI projects compatible and conclude that a tiered notion "may be preferred" — an [analysis]-grade preference, hedged in their own wording.
- [[rethinking-open-source-generative-ai#Key Quotes|Liesenfeld and Dingemanse's ACM FAccT '24 paper]] — a peer-reviewed paper for the June 2024 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency that scores 40 text generators and 6 text-to-image generators on 14 openness dimensions. Its central finding is that today's systems "can only be classified into open or closed at the price of severe information loss," so "it is no longer feasible to maintain a simple open/closed binary." Its open-washing charge is narrower: it targets licence-only or weights-only tests, and the AI Act's licence-based exemption. Their proposed middle ground: "Between radical openness and homeopathic openness lies meaningful openness."
- [[sfc-osaid-erodes-open-source#Key Quotes|Software Freedom Conservancy post]] — Kühn (October 2024) targets the OSAID's rank as well as its content: "The humble act now is to admit that it was just too soon to publish a “definition”," and he asks that the text be republished as "current recommendations." He announced a single-issue run for the OSI Board to have the OSAID repealed.
- [[what-does-open-source-ai-mean#Key Quotes|TechCrunch drafting-stage feature]] — the June 2024 origin of the naming objection. Jacks: "Neural net weights are not software source code; they are unreadable by humans, [and they are not] debuggable." Maffulli conceded the premise: "The point is correct. One of the initial debates we had was whether to call it open source AI at all, but everyone was already using the term."
- [[mozilla-celebrates-osaid#Key Quotes|Mozilla endorsement post]] — [[Mozilla]] (Ayah Bdeir, Imo Udom, Nik Marda) endorses the definition as "an important step forward" and argues a binary definition gives developers, advocates, and regulators needed precision and helps fight open-washing. The post itself calls the binary "just one of several approaches to defining open source AI."
- [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition#Key Quotes|TechCrunch release report]] — at the release, Maffulli states the standard's enforcement model: "Our hope is that when someone tries to abuse the term, the AI community will say, 'We don't recognize this as open source,' and it gets corrected." Kyle Wiggers reports the OSI has no enforcement mechanism of its own but intends to flag models that fall short, and that a committee will monitor how the OSAID is applied and propose amendments.
- [[open-future-osaid-step-forward#Key Quotes|Open Future assessment]] — Tarkowski and Keller (November 2024) record the OSI's reason for the form: it "has opted for a “binary”, non-tiered standard, arguing that it is necessary to clarify which system is compliant, and which is not." Against that, they argue that "the issue would be easiest to solve with a tiered standard," pointing to the Model Openness Framework's three tiers.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

The three objections did not replace one another; they coexist, and each is tied to a different moment. The graded objection is the oldest: the FAccT paper made it in June 2024, before the final text, and Open Future (November 2024) and Benhamou and Reymond (March 2025) restated it against the released definition. The naming objection belongs to drafting. In June 2024 Jacks said the name should not apply at all, [[StefanoMaffulli|Maffulli]] agreed with the premise, and the OSI kept the term because everyone already used it. The rank objection is specific to the October 2024 release. Kühn wanted the text demoted to recommendations. OSI co-founder Bruce Perens wanted no separate AI definition at all: the plain OSD "can be applied to AI," and the OSI should have released "a procedure on how to do that" ([[case-against-osaid|The New Stack's round-up]]). Both deny the OSAID the OSD's rank; only Kühn would keep its text as guidance.

Each camp carries a limit it admits itself:

- **The definition camp expects its line to move.** Maffulli said in June 2024 that "We can't really expect this definition to last for 26 years like the Open Source Definition" ([[what-does-open-source-ai-mean|TechCrunch drafting-stage feature]]), and the OSI calls the release a "stable version," not a final one. Who applies the line to individual systems is left unresolved. The OSI's reference page lists which systems pass and which do not, yet calls those results "not certifications of any kind" and says the OSI "will not validate or review individual AI systems" ([[osi-open-source-ai-definition|OSI reference page]]); at the release it also said it would flag models that fall short. RedMonk's Stephen O'Grady names the cost of that ambiguity: the OSD succeeded because a licence either complies or does not, while the OSAID "requires explanation to be understood" ([[osi-readies-controversial-osaid|LWN.net]]).
- **The graded critics keep a cutoff.** Liesenfeld and Dingemanse allow that graded judgements can be used "to define an openness cutoff for regulatory purposes" ([[rethinking-open-source-generative-ai|the FAccT paper]]); their quarrel is with deciding on a single feature, not with a line as such. Their paper also predates the OSAID's final text and targets licence-focused definition efforts, while the final OSAID also requires code, parameters, and data information. Benhamou and Reymond hedge their own preference ("may be preferred") and expect that, with the OSAID and the AI Act taking similar approaches, the definition of open source in AI may well stay narrowly framed regardless.
- **The repeal camp doubts any line for now.** Kühn expects it to take "at least a decade" to find the bright line of acceptable behaviour, and he says he does not yet know whether open training sets are the only way to respect user rights. His own post notes that the OSI's by-laws let the sitting Board reject election results, which limits his chosen route to repeal ([[sfc-osaid-erodes-open-source|Conservancy post]]).

The steward has used a graded vocabulary below its line since the release. The October 2024 reference page already set [[OpenWeights|open weights]] models apart, sending readers to a dedicated page, and a September 2026 OSI blog post by Katie Steen-James restates the ladder: AI systems are "closed, open-weight, or Open Source," and open weights allow "some freedoms, but not all, and only to a certain degree" ([[osi-open-weights-good-open-source-better|OSI blog]]). That is a three-rung ladder with the OSAID as the only top rung: graded words below the line, a pass/fail test at the top.

## Interpretive Direction

On this evidence the camps appear closer on mechanics than on authority. The FAccT authors accept a cutoff drawn over graded evidence, a tiered scheme such as the Model Openness Framework is itself a set of cutoffs, and the OSI has always ranked what falls below its line. What stays contested is whether one steward's line should carry the name "open source" and the standing of the OSD. The two sides ground that authority in different evidence. The OSI grounds its line in the need for a common understanding among policymakers; Kühn grounds his objection in the OSD's own origin, published in 1999 at least twelve years after industry adoption of free software and developed chiefly by Debian. The evidence on both sides is [analysis]-grade argument, each point resting on attribution to a named claimant; the corpus holds no measurement of how far the positions actually diverge in practice, or of whether a binary or a graded test better curbs open-washing. The newest argument in this dispute dates from March 2025. As of 2026-09-24 this corpus records no revision of the OSAID, no output from its amendment committee, no result of Kühn's board run, and no regulator adopting the [[ModelOpennessFramework|framework's tiers]].

Each side has a signal to watch, at least in the short term. If a single definition works, firms should drop the "open source" label for models that fail it; Maffulli reported that Google and Microsoft agreed to drop it, while [[Meta]] has not ([[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition|TechCrunch]]). If graded openness serves better, rules such as the [[EUAIAct|AI Act]]'s transparency template should move toward tiered measures. If the repeal camp's reading prevails, the OSI would republish the text as recommendations or change its board. For the wider landscape, see the [[open-source-ai-definition|Open-Source AI Definition overview]] and the synthesis on [[open-source-ai-every-camp-standard|what clears every camp's bar]].
