# What Counts as Open Source AI Under Every Camp's Standard

## Summary

No release in this corpus is documented as clearing every camp's bar for [[OpenSourceAI|open source AI]]. Ai2's OLMo, which passed the [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] (OSI)'s validation phase under that name, still comes closest, and the Swiss Apertus release of 2025-09-02 is a second candidate on its makers' own account. A 2026 essay is the only outside pass-or-fail test in the corpus that requires open data and names OLMo as passing, but it gives no version and no license terms, so OLMo's gaps have narrowed without closing. The data-required bar still has no adopted text, since the FSF's criteria remain a draft and Debian's resolution was withdrawn before a vote, and the dividing question in every camp is still [[TrainingData|training data]]: must the data itself be released on free terms, or is a description of it enough?

## 1. Every camp's bar is the union of two lists

Two stated tests set the bar. The [[osi-open-source-ai-definition|Open Source AI Definition]] (OSAID) grants the four freedoms (use, study, modify, share) through access to "the preferred form to make modifications to the system." [[open-future-osaid-step-forward|Open Future's reading of the text]] breaks that form into three conditions: detailed information about the training data, code for training and running the system under OSI-approved licenses, and model parameters, including weights, made available. The OSI's [[osi-open-weights-good-open-source-better|2026 blog post]] restates the data condition as "either the training data or a detailed account of how the data was built." The [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|Free Software Foundation]] (FSF) asks for more on data. Its [[fsf-free-ml-application-criteria|2024-10-22 statement]] requires that all the software, for both training and inference, grant the four freedoms, and that "all its training data and the related scripts for processing it" do so too. On parameters, though, the FSF says only that the four freedoms "may translate into a demand" that the release include them.

So neither list simply contains the other. The FSF is stricter on data and scripts, and the OSI is firmer on parameters. A release that satisfies every camp therefore has to meet the union of the two: free training data and processing scripts, free software, and weights that are actually released.

The two camps also demand the data on different grounds. OSI co-founder Bruce Perens holds that the training data is the source code, as [[case-against-osaid|The New Stack's round-up of critics]] reports. The FSF says the opposite: training data is not the "source code" of model parameters "in the usual sense." It demands the data anyway, because in practice people study and adapt a model by analyzing its data and retraining it. A release that cleared the union would not settle which of those reasons is right.

Other positions in the corpus sit inside the union, restate it, or were never adopted:

- **Below it** — Red Hat CTO Chris Wright sets a [[red-hat-open-source-ai-point-of-view|minimum threshold]] of open source-licensed weights plus open source software, and calls it "a starting point of open source AI, not the final destination."
- **The OSI's side, by another route** — Stanford HAI's James Landay ties [[open-weights-not-enough-open-source-science|his bar]] to the top class of the [[ModelOpennessFramework|Model Openness Framework]]: code, tooling, and the training data "or a thoroughly documented, auditable account of it." Like the OSAID, that accepts a description in place of the data.
- **The same bar, a different name** — Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller of Open Future accept the OSAID's floor but would have kept the name "open source" for systems that include open data. That is a claim about labels. The data demand it implies is already in the FSF half of the union.
- **Close to the union** — JJ Jasser and his co-authors count a model as open source only when its architecture, training code, weights and training data are all public "under licenses that permit unrestricted use, modification, and redistribution," as his [[open-washing-four-criteria|2026-07-25 Tech Policy Press essay]] puts it. Jason Corso's [[true-open-source-ai-selective-transparency|2025 VentureBeat column]] asks for a longer list of seven components, the dataset among them, with hyperparameters and random number generation added. Jasser's list omits the FSF's data-processing scripts and adds architecture; Corso's likewise names no processing scripts. Both include the data itself, so both fall on the FSF side of the split.
- **Proposed, then withdrawn** — Debian developer Mo Zhou's [[debian-ai-models-dfsg|General Resolution]] would have treated models released without their original training data or program as non-free. Thorsten Glaser's counter-proposal went further. It asked for data "legally obtained and used," data licensed for distribution, and models that can be rebuilt reproducibly, and the April report records one developer offering to second it. Zhou [[debian-ai-gr-withdrawn|withdrew his text on 2025-05-08]], saying the community was unprepared to vote, after objections that the rule would also make spam filters, OCR tools and text-to-speech software non-free. None of the counter-proposals had enough sponsors to reach the ballot, and LWN notes that Glaser had not withdrawn his.
- **An endorsement, not a bar** — [[Mozilla]] backs the OSI standard as "an important step forward" [[mozilla-celebrates-osaid|in its endorsement]], while granting that some disagree with its treatment of training data.
- **No AI definition at all** — Bradley M. Kühn of the Software Freedom Conservancy and Red Hat's Richard Fontana want the OSAID repealed. [[open-weights-not-open-source-label-dispute|The Register]] carries that call in 2026-09 without dating their statement, and the corpus records no OSAID revision and no output from its amendment committee.

The releases below are measured against the OSI and FSF tests. Glaser's text serves as an outer bound that no release here has been tested against.

## 2. What the union rules out

Most releases called "open" fall on the data side of the union. The [[open-source-ai-models-how-open|Hunton legal primer]] places [[DeepSeek]] R1 in the [[OpenWeights|open-weights]] middle ground: its weights are public under an MIT license, but its training data is not. With no data released, R1 fails the FSF half of the union on its face. [[Meta]]'s Llama 2 is among the systems that did not pass the OSI's validation phase, which the reference page says lack required components or carry legal terms at odds with open source. The OSI's rulings on Llama itself rest on the older license test, traced in [[llama-open-source-label|the Llama label dispute]].

Two of the large releases since 2025 met the license side and still fall on the data side. [[OpenAI]]'s gpt-oss shipped under Apache 2.0, and [[gpt-oss-challenges-meta-open-weight|IEEE Spectrum]]'s Matthew S. Smith reports that the release does not fulfill the OSAID's requirements on training code and data. Meta's Muse Glimmer followed in 2026-08 under Apache 2.0, and [[meta-muse-glimmer-apache-2-0|VentureBeat notes]] that Meta released neither its training data nor its training code. [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]]'s Kimi K3 is the contrast case. Its weights shipped on 2026-07-27 under a license that, as [[open-weight-diplomacy-digital-silk-road|Chinmayi Sharma reports in Lawfare]], makes large hosting businesses negotiate a separate agreement, and Jasser, writing before the release, expected its training data and training pipeline to stay closed. A standard license is therefore no longer rare among major releases, but neither Apache 2.0 release documents releasing its data.

Documenting every component is not enough on its own, because the camps also split on [[ModelLicensing|license terms]]. The [[rethinking-open-source-generative-ai|openness survey]] that Andreas Liesenfeld and Mark Dingemanse presented at FAccT '24, the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency, scores a system as fully licensed when an OSI license or a Responsible AI License (RAIL) covers it. Its walkthrough of BloomZ against Llama concludes that "Only BloomZ can substantially claim open source status." BloomZ, from the BigScience Workshop team, releases its code under Apache 2.0 and its weights under RAIL. Open Future, by contrast, reads the OSAID as excluding systems under responsible AI licenses, because those licenses restrict certain uses. The OSI's own reference page lists BLOOM, from the same BigScience team, among the models that "would probably pass if they changed their licenses/legal terms." So the survey's leading example of open source status comes from a team whose model the OSI says would probably pass but for its license terms. That is why the license is one of the gaps for OLMo below.

## 3. OLMo and Apertus: components documented, not every link

The [[hello-olmo-truly-open-llm|Ai2 announcement]] of 2024-02-01 lists what shipped with OLMo 7B:

- **Data and scripts** — the Dolma corpus of three trillion tokens, "including code that produces the training data."
- **Weights and training software** — full weights for four variants at the 7B scale, each trained to at least 2 trillion tokens, with inference code, training metrics and training logs.
- **Evaluation** — the development evaluation suite, with more than 500 checkpoints per model.

That list covers data, processing scripts, training software and weights, which is every item in the union. The source is Ai2's own announcement, though, and it assesses Ai2's "truly open" claim against no outside test. The release also came about nine months before either test existed: the FSF statement is dated 2024-10-22, and the OSAID 1.0 was [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition|released on 2024-10-28]].

[[apertus-fully-open-multilingual-llm|Apertus]] makes a second claim of the same kind. EPFL, ETH Zurich and the Swiss National Supercomputing Centre say its architecture, weights, training data and training methods are all "openly accessible and fully documented," at 8B and 70B parameters. What they list as released under "the permissive open-source license," which allows commercial use and whose terms sit on [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]], is the documentation and source code of the training process and the datasets used, plus the weights with intermediate checkpoints. The announcement does not state the terms of the training data itself. It also says the training corpus "builds only on data which is publicly available," filtered for website opt-outs and personal data. Publicly available is not the same as licensed for free redistribution, which is what the FSF test and Glaser's text turn on. The corpus records no OSI, FSF or other outside ruling on Apertus, so it carries the same gaps as OLMo with less outside comment.

Three gaps stand between OLMo's components and a finding that it clears every camp's bar; Apertus shares the second and third, and adds the data-licensing question above.

**Which OLMo.** The OSI's reference page lists "OLMo (AI2)" among the models that passed without naming a version. The OSI's 2026 blog post says a memorization study was "only possible because Olmo is released as Open Source AI," again without a version. Jasser's essay names "The Allen Institute's OLMo" among the handful of models that meet his four criteria, also without a version. The FAccT survey ranks "OLMo Instruct" near full openness, a name the Ai2 announcement does not use. The [[eu-ai-act-gpai-guide-open-source-developers|EU AI Act guide]] by Hugging Face, Mozilla and Linux Foundation researchers cites OLMo 2, a later release the corpus holds no announcement for. The model the New Stack round-up calls "AMD's OLMo" is AMD's own 1-billion-parameter release, not Ai2's. No source in the corpus ties the model that passed validation to the 2024-02-01 release that documents open data.

**Which license.** The FSF test turns on whether data and scripts "respect all users, following the four freedoms," and the OSI requires code under OSI-approved licenses. Both are questions about license terms. The Ai2 announcement says what it released and where to get it (on Hugging Face and GitHub), but not under what terms. Two later sources come nearer without closing the gap. The EU guide classes OLMo 2 as a model under a free and open-source license, but that is the AI Act's definition, not the OSI's or the FSF's, and it concerns a later model. Jasser's criteria require unrestricted licenses on all four components, so naming OLMo implies its terms pass, but he does not say what they are. Section 2 shows that license terms alone can split the camps. The one license written to cover weights, code, documentation and data in a single grant, the Linux Foundation's [[OpenMDW]], has not cleared the OSI either: its review [[lwn-openmdw-license-review|wound down on the OSI list]] in 2026-08 with the OSI, in LWN's reading, not ready to approve it.

**Who applies the test.** As of its 2024-10-22 statement the FSF's working group was still drafting the exact text of its criteria, and the corpus holds no final text. The one FSF finding on a model that the corpus records is second-hand: the OSI's [[osi-meta-llama-license-still-not-open-source|2025-02-18 post on Llama]] agrees with an FSF evaluation that the Llama 3.1 Community License fails freedom 0, the freedom to use the model for any purpose. That is a ruling on a license under the software freedoms, not an application of the machine-learning criteria. The OSI, for its part, says its validation results "should be seen as part of the definitional process" and are "not certifications of any kind," and that it "will not validate or review individual AI systems." It does intend, TechCrunch reports, to flag models described as open source that fall short, and it has set up a committee to monitor how the OSAID is applied.

Jasser's essay is now the only outside pass-or-fail test requiring open data that the corpus records being applied to OLMo; the FAccT survey grades openness rather than passing or failing it. It is a single author's opinion piece applying his own co-authored criteria, and it gives a verdict without a version or license terms. Neither the OSI nor the FSF names a body that confirms a pass. What OLMo lacks is a documented pass under either stated bar, not a party able to object. The same gap in the OSAID's design is followed in [[osaid-form-and-legitimacy|the dispute over the definition's form and legitimacy]].

The other models that passed validation stand further back. Pythia, CrystalCoder and T5 have no open-data release recorded in the corpus, and passing the OSAID turns on data *information*, so it cannot stand in for one. LLM360's Amber comes a step closer. It passed validation by name, and the FAccT survey says the organizations behind AmberChat, OLMo Instruct and BloomZ "have gone to great lengths to make training data, code, training pipelines, and documentation available." The corpus holds no release record for Amber itself. Jasser also names EleutherAI's GPT-NeoX and LLM360's K2 as meeting his criteria, and the corpus holds no release record for either.

## 4. What the answer settles, and what would close the gap

The answer is narrow. No release here is documented, with a version and its license terms, as meeting the union of the OSI and FSF tests. OLMo is where the gap is smallest, and there the gap is in the record, not in the components: a version, a license statement, and a body that applies the test. Apertus stands at about the same point on its makers' own account, with its data's licensing a further open question. A license statement for Dolma and the OLMo code, together with a final FSF text applied to a named release, would close most of OLMo's gap. The OSI has said it will not rule on individual systems, so the version gap on its side may stay open.

The bars themselves are less settled than when the OSAID shipped. Debian's attempt to adopt a data rule ended in withdrawal, the corpus still holds no final FSF text, and on the other side the repeal call noted in section 1 stands unanswered in the corpus. A release judged today would therefore be judged against the OSAID 1.0 text and the FSF's draft criteria, with no adopted open-data text beside them.

The answer does not reach the scale question. The open-data releases in the New Stack round-up answer the objection that there is "no good example of an open data LLM." [[EleutherAI]]'s [[eleutherai-common-pile-dataset|Common Pile]] of 2025-06-06 goes further on the data's terms. It is 8 terabytes of openly licensed and public-domain text, and EleutherAI says two 7-billion-parameter Comma models trained on part of it rival Meta's first Llama model. Those benchmark claims are EleutherAI's own, and the corpus records the Comma models as trained, not their release terms. At 70B parameters, Apertus is the largest release claiming full openness that the corpus gives a size for. Open Future's point that open resources lack the volume and diversity to train large foundation models is argued in [[open-training-data-requirement|the open training-data dispute]], and this page does not settle it.
