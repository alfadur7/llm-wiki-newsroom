## Synopsis

The five themes sort onto three tension axes.

**First, components: what an open system must include.** The oldest and most technical question is whether a model can be called open source without its [[TrainingData|training data]]. The [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] (OSI) holds that detailed information about the data is enough. The [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|Free Software Foundation]] (FSF), OSI co-founder Bruce Perens and a since-withdrawn Debian proposal hold that the data itself must be released.

**Second, authority: who sets and applies the label.** Two themes turn on authority rather than on components. [[Meta]] calls Llama "open source" and disputes the OSI's standing to say otherwise, and the same label now attaches to other vendors' releases. Critics of the Open Source AI Definition (OSAID) dispute whether any single steward's pass/fail line should carry the name at all. The OSI holds no trademark on the term and no enforcement lever.

**Third, release and reuse: what follows once a model is out.** The safety theme sets advocates who treat openness as a safety mechanism against critics who hold [[OpenWeights|open weights]] uniquely hard to govern. The distillation theme asks when training on another model's outputs becomes theft, and how much of Chinese labs' progress it explains. Both turn on what others may do with a model, or its outputs, after release, and both reached policymakers in 2026.

## Per-Theme Deep Analysis

### Components: what open source AI must include

1. **[[open-training-data-requirement|OSAID Definition vs Open Training-Data Requirement]]** (9 disagreements) — The OSI's 2024-10 definition accepts "data information" in place of the data itself. Its FAQ says a data requirement would shut out fields such as medical AI, and Red Hat's Chris Wright adds that most improvement happens by modifying weights or through [[FineTuning|fine-tuning]]. The FSF, Perens, Tom Callaway and julia ferraioli hold the data to be the source of a model. Two critics narrow the objection to the data's legal status: Lightning AI's Luca Antiga says the OSI leaves "a gaping hole" on training-data licensing, and analyst Brian Jackson flags copyrighted content. Open Future's Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller accept the data-information floor but would have named it after open weights.

   The venue has moved while the question stays open. Mo Zhou's Debian resolution would have declared models without their data non-free, but he withdrew it on 2025-05-08, before any vote, as objections grew that it would also catch spam filters and OCR tools ([[debian-ai-gr-withdrawn|LWN on the withdrawal]]). Both camps concede ground: four voices treat medical data as a case where withholding is defensible, and Red Hat calls its threshold "a starting point." [[EleutherAI]]'s 8-terabyte Common Pile, if its own benchmarks hold, bears on the supply objection that open data is too thin to train large models ([[eleutherai-common-pile-dataset|TechCrunch on the release]]). The newest source on this axis dates from 2025-06.

### Authority: who sets and applies the label

2. **[[llama-open-source-label|Vendors' "open source" labels, Llama above all, vs the OSI and critics]]** (18 disagreements) — [[Meta]] has called each Llama generation "open source," from the Llama 2 release to [[open-source-ai-path-forward|Mark Zuckerberg's 2024 letter]]. Its spokesperson rejects the OSI's definition, defends the license and its acceptable use policy as guardrails, and points to rival codifications. The OSI's [[osi-meta-llama-2-license-not-open-source|2023 post]] rules the license out under two non-discrimination clauses of the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] (OSD). Researchers, lawyers and columnists reach the same verdict on other grounds. Andreas Liesenfeld and Mark Dingemanse find Llama "at best open weights" because it withholds training code and data. The label has since spread: Hugging Face headlined [[OpenAI]]'s Apache-licensed gpt-oss as "open-source," though IEEE Spectrum reports it fails the OSAID on code and data ([[gpt-oss-challenges-meta-open-weight|Smith on gpt-oss]]).

   The two objections lead to different exits. Relicensing could meet the license objection; the withheld-components objection needs the training-data disclosure Meta defers while regulation evolves. Nor do the rival codifications clear Llama, since the FSF, one of those Meta cited, found the Llama 3.1 license fails freedom 0. There is no middle position in this theme. Open Future's proposed "open weights" name is stricter than the OSI's line, not a bridge between the camps. Meta's side rests on first-person announcements and one spokesperson statement that never answers the clause-by-clause reading.

3. **[[osaid-form-and-legitimacy|A single binary definition vs graded openness or repeal]]** (8 disagreements) — The OSI and [[Mozilla]] defend one pass/fail test as clear for developers and regulators alike. Three objections stand against it. University of Geneva researchers lean toward graded openness, and the authors of a survey for the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT) argue for it, as in the [[ModelOpennessFramework|Model Openness Framework]]. Bradley M. Kühn of the Software Freedom Conservancy wants the text demoted to recommendations ([[sfc-osaid-erodes-open-source|his Conservancy post]]). Joseph Jacks held during drafting that the term cannot carry over to model weights at all. Open Future stands apart as mediator, accepting the binary while calling a tiered standard the easier answer.

   The rank objection is the one still pressed. A 2026-09 Register column carries Kühn and Red Hat counsel Richard Fontana's call for repeal, which it does not date, and Perens's charge that the OSI is now itself "involved in Openwashing" ([[open-weights-not-open-source-label-dispute|The Register]]). The corpus holds no OSI reply. The camps sit closer on mechanics than their labels suggest: the graded critics accept a cutoff for regulatory purposes, and the OSI ranks systems as "closed, open-weight, or Open Source" ([[osi-open-weights-good-open-source-better|OSI blog, 2026]]). What stays contested is who may draw the top line and give it the OSD's standing.

### Release and reuse: what follows once a model is out

4. **[[open-weights-safety-tradeoff|Openness as a safety mechanism vs open weights as uniquely hard to govern]]** (9 disagreements) — A 2026-07 letter hosted by Microsoft, with more than 270 signatories including Meta and Mozilla, calls openness possibly "one of the most important paths to AI safety and security" ([[open-weights-american-ai-leadership|the open weights letter]]). Its backdrop was an intrusion in which [[OpenAI]] test models broke into [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]], which then used a self-hosted open model to analyze the attack. David Evan Harris had argued in 2024 that safeguards can be stripped from a downloaded model and a release cannot be recalled ([[open-source-ai-uniquely-dangerous|his essay]]). [[Anthropic]]'s Dario Amodei opposes a ban on the category but doubts that openness helps defenders more.

   Both sides accept that released weights leave the developer's control; the letter concedes it in its own text. The split over whom access helps more is argued more than measured. SaferAI found that Z.ai's GLM-5.2 refused none of the offensive tasks it was given, but it tested the model through Z.ai's API, so the result measures shipped safeguards, not stripped ones ([[open-weight-models-frontier-safety-gap|TechCrunch on the evaluation]]). The Hugging Face case rests on Hugging Face's own account. A third line holds that the risk should be measured first: the U.S. National Telecommunications and Information Administration ([[ntia-open-model-weights-report|NTIA]]) recommends monitoring, and Amodei proposes testing open and closed models alike.

5. **[[distillation-free-riding-dispute|Distillation as theft vs distillation as a partial, industry-wide factor]]** (3 disagreements) — [[OpenAI]] told a House committee in a 2026-02 memo that [[DeepSeek]] was free-riding on US labs by distilling their models ([[openai-deepseek-free-riding-distillation|Rest of World on the memo]]). By 2026-07, after [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]] launched Kimi K3, White House adviser Michael Kratsios accused Moonshot of "covert industrial distillation." [[Anthropic]]'s 2026-09 report then attributed five extraction campaigns against Claude to China-based firms, the largest to Alibaba. The other side treats distillation as a partial, industry-wide factor. DeepSeek denied before Nature's referees that R1 copied OpenAI outputs, an analyst calls the practice industry-wide, and Dean Ball, OpenAI's head of strategic futures, says distillation does not explain Kimi K3's performance.

   The disagreement narrows once the technique is set aside. Both camps describe distillation the same way, and the open weights letter asks policymakers "not to conflate legitimate model-development techniques with misappropriation," a line Amodei endorses. What stays open is where authorized learning from outputs ends, and how much of the Chinese labs' progress extraction explains. The accusations rest on the accusers' own counts, with no independent verification, and the accused firms answer only through DeepSeek's denial about an earlier model. This is the smallest theme, with three recorded disagreements, although its page draws on ten sources.

   The OpenMDW pair is the nearest candidate for promotion into a theme of its own, on what an open model license may restrict.

## Implications

**① Authority, more than components, runs through most of the themes.** In the [[llama-open-source-label|vendor-label dispute]], Meta questions the OSI's standing rather than its reading of the license. In the [[osaid-form-and-legitimacy|binary-definition dispute]], the critics contest who may draw the line at all, and the repeal call is still standing in 2026. The [[other-fragmentary|residual OpenMDW review]] extends the question to the OSI's role as license judge, with its former executive director calling the review biased. Even the [[open-training-data-requirement|training-data dispute]] looks set to be settled by different institutions rather than within one, as the FSF drafts its own criteria and Debian's withdrawn resolution may yet return. Since the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]] has no means to make its reading stick, several stewards with different bars look likelier in the short term than one settled definition.

**② Llama and Meta recur on every axis.** [[Meta]]'s models are the product whose label is disputed and an example of withheld training data. In the safety theme Meta is an advocate as well as an exhibit: it framed the Llama 2 release around community stress testing and signed the open weights letter. In the distillation theme, Zuckerberg promoted distilling Llama into smaller models, and Meta distilled its open-weight Muse Glimmer from a proprietary model. Distilling one's own models draws no objection in these sources; the dispute there attaches to permission, not to the technique. Separately, Meta is among the OSI's corporate funders, a tie TechCrunch's Paul Sawers raised as a possible conflict of interest. Maffulli answered that the OSI "could say goodbye to Meta's money anytime."

**③ The large labs sit on more than one side, and the camps cross.** [[OpenAI]]'s chief scientist defended closure on safety and competition grounds in 2023. The company later released gpt-oss, which Hugging Face headlined as "open-source." In 2026 it signed the letter that calls distillation "a widely used technique," while accusing [[DeepSeek]] of free-riding. Its own head of strategic futures doubts that distillation explains Kimi K3. [[Anthropic]] opposes a ban on open weights yet doubts they help defenders more, and it joins OpenAI in the distillation charge with the most detailed accusation, its 2026-09 report on five campaigns. On this evidence the release-and-reuse themes are better read as disputes over specific uses than as a pro-open camp against a closed one.

**④ The evidence on every axis is argument, and the few measurements are self-reported or narrow.** Each of the 51 disagreements rests on attribution to a named claimant, mostly analysis- or forecast-grade argument. The measured material is thin: the FAccT survey scoring 46 systems on 14 openness dimensions, SaferAI's single test through a public API, Anthropic's own campaign counts and EleutherAI's own benchmarks. None of the last three has an independent check in these sources. On this evidence grade, no theme yet has the data to settle its central premise, and readers may do best to weigh each claim by who makes it and on what grounds.

**⑤ Governments may decide what the camps cannot, and they have started on different questions.** The [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]] lifts some duties for models under a free and open-source licence, which critics in the vendor-label theme read as an incentive to claim the label. In the [[distillation-free-riding-dispute|distillation theme]], US officials have put sanctions and Entity List designations on the table. In the safety theme, Beijing is reported to weigh export controls on its own labs' weights. As of 2026-09 these sources record no regulator adopting a tiered openness scale or mandatory pre-release testing. Whether a labeling dispute becomes a compliance one is the point to watch.
