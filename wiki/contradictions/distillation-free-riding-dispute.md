# Distillation as theft vs distillation as a partial, industry-wide factor

## Opposing Positions

The sources agree on what [[Distillation|distillation]] is: training one model on the outputs of another. They split over when the practice becomes theft, and over how much of Chinese labs' progress it explains. US frontier labs and US officials describe the large-scale harvesting of closed models' outputs by Chinese rivals as free-riding on American research. The accused lab denies the charge for its own model, one analyst calls the practice industry-wide, and other commentators doubt that it explains the performance of Chinese models.

**Position A (distillation of closed models as free-riding)**: In a memo to the U.S. House Select Committee on China, [[OpenAI]] wrote that "DeepSeek's next model (whatever its form) should be understood in the context of its ongoing efforts to free-ride on the capabilities developed by OpenAI and other US frontier labs." It said accounts tied to [[DeepSeek]] employees masked their source through third-party routers and pulled outputs "for distillation in programmatic ways." By 2026-07, after [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]] launched Kimi K3, White House science adviser Michael Kratsios wrote on X that Moonshot had built an internal platform for large-scale distillation against US models, and he called "covert industrial distillation aimed at stealing proprietary U.S. technology" unacceptable. [[Anthropic]] followed in 2026-09 with a report attributing five extraction campaigns against its Claude models to China-based companies, the largest of them to Alibaba. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei gives the strategic case: distillation lets China partially evade chip bans and can bring its frontier to within a few months of the US frontier.

**Position B (distillation as a partial, industry-wide factor)**: This camp holds three strands. [[DeepSeek]] denies the fact for R1: it told the referees of its peer-reviewed paper that the model did not learn by copying reasoning examples generated by [[OpenAI]] models, and it credits pure reinforcement learning instead. Counterpoint's Neil Shah calls the practice industry-wide: "none of the models is an island and the entire industry has mostly evolved based on recursive learning." Dean Ball and Nathan Lambert dispute its magnitude. Lawfare columnist Tom Uren, who doubts that distillation is "the whole ballgame," cites Ball saying that distillation does not fully account for the performance of Moonshot AI's Kimi K3, and Lambert arguing that distillation's impact is overstated and becoming less important.

**Position C (separate the technique from unlawful extraction)**: The open weights letter asks policymakers "not to conflate legitimate model-development techniques with misappropriation." It would handle unlawful extraction through "targeted legal and commercial frameworks" rather than sweeping limits on distillation. Amodei says he agrees and counts his own call to crack down on "industrial-scale" operations as that kind of measure.

## Representative Evidence

- [[openai-deepseek-free-riding-distillation#Key Quotes|Rest of World on the OpenAI memo]] — [[OpenAI]]'s memo of 2026-02-12 states: "We have observed accounts associated with DeepSeek employees developing methods to circumvent OpenAI's access restrictions and access models through obfuscated third-party routers and other ways that mask their source." OpenAI adds that it does not permit its outputs to be used for "imitation frontier AI models." The article carries no response from [[DeepSeek]]. Of its two outside voices, Shah questions the framing and RAND's Austin Horng-En Wang questions the timing.
- [[anthropic-distillation-campaigns-alibaba-moonshot#Key Claims|TechCrunch on Anthropic's distillation report]] — [[Anthropic]] counts nearly 200 million exchanges across five campaigns aimed at Claude's chain of thought. It links 151 million of them, run from 2026-05 to 2026-07 across 3,500 accounts, to training material for Alibaba's Qwen models, on the ground that they shared one fixed extraction prompt. Every figure is Anthropic's own count, and the article reports no response from Alibaba or [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]].
- [[secrets-of-deepseek-r1-landmark-paper#Key Claims|Nature on the R1 peer review]] — [[DeepSeek]] denied before referees that R1 copied [[OpenAI]]'s reasoning examples, but, in Nature's account, acknowledged that its web-trained base model will have taken in whatever AI-generated content was already online. Ohio State's Huan Sun calls the rebuttal "as convincing as what we could see in any publication." [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]]'s Lewis Tunstall, a reviewer of the paper, cannot be fully sure. He adds that replication attempts by other labs suggest the recipe is probably good enough not to need OpenAI outputs.
- [[knives-out-open-weight-ai-models#Key Claims|Uren, "Knives Are Out for Open-Weight AI Models"]] — Kratsios accuses [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]] of building "a sophisticated internal platform to conduct large scale distillation against U.S. models," and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says sanctions and Entity List designations "will be on the table." In the same column, Ball says Kimi K3's performance cannot "be explained away by distillation." Lambert argues that distillation's impact is overstated and that evolving training pipelines are making it less important.
- [[anthropic-position-open-weights-models#Key Claims|Amodei, "Our position on open-weights models"]] — Amodei writes that distillation "does not allow the CCP to obtain equivalent or superior AI capabilities to the US," but can close the gap to a few months. He says [[Anthropic]] bans the accounts it identifies. Those accounts are often found only after substantial distillation has occurred, which is why he asks for policy rather than company practice alone.
- [[open-weights-american-ai-leadership#Key Claims|Open Weights and American AI Leadership]] — the letter's signatories, who include [[OpenAI]], [[Meta]] and [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]], call distillation "a widely used technique for model improvement, evaluation, and validation" that reflects "a long tradition of learning from, building upon, and improving existing technologies." They grant that "unlawful efforts to extract value from closed models raise legitimate concerns."
- [[deepseek-r1-release#Key Quotes|DeepSeek-R1 Release]] — the earliest item in the dispute, from 2025-01. [[DeepSeek]] moved R1 to the MIT License with the line "Distill & commercialize freely!" It also released six small models distilled from R1 and opened R1's API outputs to [[FineTuning|fine-tuning]] and distillation.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

The dispute escalated in scale and venue over some twenty months. In 2025-01 it was a single suspicion about a single model: media reports said [[OpenAI]] researchers thought R1 had been trained on OpenAI outputs, and OpenAI investigated. By the 2025-09 peer review, DeepSeek's denial had entered the scientific record. In 2026-02 OpenAI carried the charge to Congress, aimed now at a model that had not yet launched, and [[Anthropic]] began naming labs the same month. RAND's Wang, without disputing the charge, asked why OpenAI escalated then and offered one possible motive: keeping chips from Chinese firms. By 2026-07 the accusers included US officials threatening sanctions, and the named targets had widened from DeepSeek to Moonshot AI; by 2026-09, Alibaba. The pairing in this theme is also reconstructed rather than exchanged. DeepSeek's denial answers the 2025 suspicion about R1, not the 2026 memo, which none of these sources records DeepSeek answering. Uren's column never mentions Amodei.

Several actors appear on both sides. OpenAI signed the open weights letter, which calls distillation widely used and warns against conflating it with misappropriation. Uren identifies Dean Ball, one of the sceptics, as OpenAI's head of strategic futures. "Interconnects AI," which matches the name of Lambert's newsletter, also appears among the letter's signatories. Distillation of one's own models draws no objection in these sources. [[meta-muse-glimmer-apache-2-0|Meta]] distilled its [[OpenWeights|open-weight]] Muse Glimmer from the proprietary Muse Spark. In [[open-source-ai-path-forward|his 2024 letter]], [[MarkZuckerberg|Mark Zuckerberg]] promoted distilling Llama into smaller models as a benefit of open release. In these sources the objections attach to permission, not to the technique. DeepSeek granted permission for R1, OpenAI withholds it, and [[open-model-licenses-concerning-restrictions|Gretel's Eric Tramel]] notes that model licenses write specific carve-outs for distillation into their terms (see [[ModelLicensing|model licensing]]).

Both camps' evidence has limits. The accusations rest on the accusers' own observations: OpenAI's account of router use and Anthropic's campaign counts have no independent verification in these sources, and the accused firms' voices are absent from them. [[DeepSeek]], Alibaba and [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]] appear on the distillation charge only through their accusers or, in DeepSeek's case, through a denial about an earlier model. TechCrunch reports that the Moonshot campaign seemed to route requests directly from the Chinese military, and gives as its example one request to judge whether a subject in surveillance footage was "behaving abnormally"; that link rests on Anthropic's report alone. On the other side, DeepSeek has not published R1's [[TrainingData|training data]], so its denial cannot be checked against the data itself. Its own concession about AI-generated web content also means some indirect exposure to other models' outputs is likely. Amodei and Ball are also closer than the pairing suggests. Amodei says distillation cannot deliver equal capability, and Ball says Kimi K3's performance cannot be explained away by it. They differ on magnitude, not on whether distillation is the whole story.

## Interpretive Direction

On these sources, the disagreement is less about the technique, which both camps describe the same way and several accusers use themselves, than about two narrower questions. The first is where authorized learning from outputs ends and extraction from a closed model begins. The second is how much of the Chinese labs' progress that extraction explains. Neither question is settled here. The theft framing is backed by specific but unverified company counts, and the sceptics' case is argued rather than measured. The policy response has moved faster than independent verification: a company memo in 2026-02, sanctions talk by 2026-07. In the short term, Position C's language is the one both the letter's signatories and Amodei endorse, although they disagree on what it permits.

The monitoring points are symmetric. For Position A, the test is whether an independent party confirms the scale of the campaigns, or whether the accused firms answer the attributions. For Position B, the test is whether the gap between open and closed frontiers holds as distillation defenses tighten. Amodei's account predicts that cutting off distillation would widen it, and Lambert's predicts little change. For Position C, the signal is whether "targeted legal and commercial frameworks" take concrete form, or whether US action arrives as Entity List designations that Uren's column reports as already on the table.
