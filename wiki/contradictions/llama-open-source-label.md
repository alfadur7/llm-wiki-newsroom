# Meta's 'open source' label for Llama vs the OSI and critics' rejection

## Opposing Positions

[[Meta]] has called each generation of its Llama models "open source," from Llama 2 in July 2023 to Llama 3.1 a year later. The [[OpenSourceInitiative]] (OSI) and a line of independent researchers reject the label. They hold that Llama's license limits who may use the model and for what, and that the release withholds training code and data. The clash is over who sets the meaning of the term for AI models: the company that ships them, or the steward of the software definition.

**Position A** (Meta: the label describes a free release that ships its weights): Meta's own announcements carry this side in the first person. The [[llama-2-meta-microsoft|Llama 2 announcement]] calls the model "the next generation of our open source large language model," free for research and commercial use, with [[OpenWeights|weights]] and starting code. Mark Zuckerberg's [[open-source-ai-path-forward|2024 letter]] calls Llama 3.1 405B "the first frontier-level open source AI model" and compares Llama's path to Linux overtaking closed Unix. When the OSI published its definition, a Meta spokesperson said Meta disagrees with it: "There is no single open source AI definition." The spokesperson also defended the license and its acceptable use policy as guardrails against harmful deployments. On the withheld components, Meta says it is taking a "cautious approach" to sharing model details, including training data, while regulations such as California's training transparency law evolve. Against the OSI's authority, the spokesperson pointed to other codifications: the Linux Foundation's suggested definitions, the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation]]'s criteria for free machine learning applications, and proposals from other researchers. Within this corpus, Meta never answers the OSI's clause-by-clause reading of the license. Its defense is that software-era definitions do not fit AI, that its restrictions serve [[AISafety|safety]], and that disclosure waits on regulation.

**Position B** (OSI and critics: the license and the withheld components decide): The OSI's [[osi-meta-llama-2-license-not-open-source|2023 post]] by [[StefanoMaffulli]] rules the Llama 2 license out under [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] (OSD) points 5 and 6, which bar discrimination against persons, groups, or fields of endeavor. It names two offending terms: "restrictions on commercial use for some users (paragraph 2)" and the purpose bans in the Acceptable Use Policy. In 2025 the OSI said Llama 3.x "is still not Open Source by any stretch of the imagination." Researchers reach the same verdict on other grounds. They find Llama "at best open weights," closed on training code, data, and documentation.

## Representative Evidence

- [[osi-meta-llama-2-license-not-open-source#Key Quotes|OSI, July 2023]] — the historical origin of the dispute. Maffulli writes that "Meta is confusing 'open source' with 'resources available to some users under some conditions,'" and says the OSI asked Meta to correct the statement. He also concedes the other side's motive: the OSI "does not question Meta's desire to limit the use of Llama for competitive purposes."
- [[llama-2-meta-microsoft#Key Claims|Meta, Llama 2 release]] — Meta's [fact]-grade account of what it shipped: model weights and starting code for pretrained and [[FineTuning|fine-tuned]] versions, free of charge, paired with an Acceptable Use Policy that prohibits certain use cases. The post reproduces no license terms, so the label rests on the release rather than on the grant.
- [[llama-3-1-community-license#Key Quotes|Llama 3.1 Community License]] — the [[ModelLicensing|license]] text behind the later label. It grants a royalty-free right to use, modify, and redistribute. It also requires "Llama" at the start of any derived model's name, binds all use to the Acceptable Use Policy, and withholds the grant from licensees above 700 million monthly active users unless Meta agrees.
- [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition#Key Quotes|TechCrunch on the OSI definition, October 2024]] — the only source where Meta answers the definition. Its spokesperson says "we, like others across the industry, disagree with their new definition," despite Meta's part in drafting it, and cites evolving regulation for its caution over training-data details. Maffulli says Google and Microsoft dropped the term for models that are not fully open after talks with the OSI, but Meta has not.
- [[rethinking-open-source-generative-ai#Key Claims|Liesenfeld and Dingemanse, FAccT '24]] — a peer-reviewed survey, presented at the ACM FAccT fairness and accountability conference, of 40 text generators and 6 text-to-image systems scored on 14 openness dimensions. It concludes that "Meta's Llama is at best open weights" and that Llama 3 "is no different from Llama 2 in terms of openness." Its grounds are components, not license: no training code, a one-line description of the base data, and weights behind a consent form. The authors also warn that the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]]'s exemption for models under a free and open licence makes the label worth claiming, since it lifts documentation duties.
- [[what-does-open-source-ai-mean#Key Quotes|TechCrunch, June 2024]] — Maffulli claims consensus against the label: "Everyone else that is involved in the conversation is perfectly agreeing that Llama itself cannot be considered open source," adding that people at Meta "know that it's a little bit of a stretch." The attribution is his alone; no Meta employee is quoted.
- [[osi-meta-llama-license-still-not-open-source#Key Claims|OSI, February 2025]] — Jordan Maris finds the Llama 3.x licenses still fail OSD points 5 and 6. He endorses the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation]]'s view that they fail freedom 0, and reports that newer versions exclude persons in the European Union. He also argues the OSI's AI definition is not needed to reach this verdict.

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

**The critics agree on the verdict but not on the test.** The OSI's two blog posts make a license case. Maris says the [[OpenSourceAI|Open Source AI Definition]] (OSAID) is not needed to judge Llama, because the old software freedoms already fail. The FAccT researchers make a component case instead. They warn that basing openness on a single measure, such as the mere availability of weights or an open licence, is one of the most reductive methods and an easy route to [[OpenWashing]], and they name the OSI's own consultation as too focused on licensing. The split matters for any exit. Meta could in principle meet the OSI's OSD objection by rewriting the license, though the OSI's OSAID validation would still ask for information on the data. It could not meet the researchers' objection without also releasing training code and [[TrainingData]] detail, the very disclosure on which Meta says it is taking a "cautious approach" while regulation evolves. Its appeal to other codifications does not escape the license case either: the Free Software Foundation, whose criteria the spokesperson cited, found the Llama 3.1 license fails freedom 0, and the OSI's February 2025 post endorses that finding. Open Future's Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller [[open-future-osaid-step-forward|combine both grounds]], rejecting the label for "non-OSI compliant licenses" and for carrying no information on the training data.

**Meta's wording has moved, but not away from the label.** Paul Sawers reports that with Llama 3 in April 2024 Meta shifted toward "openly available" and "openly accessible," while still saying "open source" in places. The FAccT paper quotes the Llama 3 blog calling it "open source" all the same. By July 2024 the Zuckerberg letter uses the term outright, and the letter is filed here as contradicting the OSI's 2023 request to correct it. So the retreat Sawers saw proved partial and short, at least in the documents this corpus holds.

**The steward has no lever, and Meta is among its funders.** The OSI has no enforcement mechanism; it can only flag models that fall short. Bradley M. Kühn of the Software Freedom Conservancy argues that because the OSI never secured a trademark for "open source," it [[sfc-osaid-erodes-open-source|cannot stop Zuckerberg]] from using the term for Llama. Both TechCrunch pieces also report that [[Meta]] is among the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]]'s corporate funders. Sawers raises the possible conflict of interest, and [[StefanoMaffulli|Maffulli]] answers that the OSI "could say goodbye to Meta's money anytime."

**Llama became an argument inside the definition fight.** Open Future reads Meta's public disagreement as evidence that the OSAID already pressures firms not to call partly open models "open source." The OSI, for its part, accuses unnamed critics of the definition of tying their complaints to Meta's use of the term, and says the dispute predates the OSAID entirely. One qualifier limits the OSI's side: its [[osi-open-source-ai-definition|validation list]], which placed Llama 2 among systems that do not pass, is framed on the OSI's own page as part of the definitional process and "not certifications of any kind."

## Interpretive Direction

On the evidence in this corpus, the dispute is not converging. Meta's side rests on first-person announcements and one spokesperson statement; the critics' side rests on a published clause-by-clause reading, a scored survey, and the OSI's repeated demands. That asymmetry is one of form as much as weight. Meta disputes the definition's authority rather than the reading of its own license, so the two sides do not meet on a shared test. Read the opposition as a labeling dispute between named parties, not as a measured factual conflict, and weigh each claim by who makes it and on what evidence.

In the short term, four points are worth monitoring. First, whether Meta relicenses a later Llama under terms that clear OSD points 5 and 6; the corpus currently records no Meta reply to the OSI's 2025 post. Second, whether the OSI's flag-without-enforcement approach moves Meta the way Maffulli says it moved Google and Microsoft. Third, the test of Meta's own position: whether the alternative codifications it points to, or the EU AI Act's open-source definition, which Open Future notes does not require training data to be shared, come to accept Llama-style releases as open, and whether the "others across the industry" Meta invokes keep using the term. Fourth, whether the AI Act's exemption turns a branding dispute into a compliance one. The license terms themselves are tracked under [[ModelLicensing]]. The wider landscape sits in the [[open-source-ai-definition|Open-Source AI Definition]] and [[open-weights|Open Weights]] overviews, which weigh whether Llama is better called an open-weights release.
