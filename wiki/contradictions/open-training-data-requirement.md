# OSAID Definition vs Open Training-Data Requirement

## Opposing Positions

The [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] (OSI) released its Open Source AI Definition (OSAID) in October 2024. The definition lets a system count as open source without releasing its [[TrainingData|training data]], provided it discloses detailed "data information" about that data. The dispute turns on one technical question: is training data part of the "preferred form for making modifications" — the test the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] applies to source code — or a useful extra? One camp answers that data is a benefit and weights plus code are what people modify. The other answers that data is the source of a model, so a definition that makes it optional is not open source. The wider landscape of the definition is surveyed in [[open-source-ai-definition|the Open-Source AI Definition overview]].

**Position A — training data as a benefit**: The OSI stated in its 0.0.9 draft that training data "is not part of the preferred form" because its insights "have already been learned." Its FAQ defends the choice as what lets open source AI exist where data cannot legally be shared, such as medical AI. OSI executive director [[StefanoMaffulli|Stefano Maffulli]] rejects the charge that this lowers the bar. He attributes the preferred-form finding to the definition's endorsers and to Carnegie Mellon University's comment, not to himself or the OSI board, and argues that neither the more-components approach nor the weights-and-architecture approach is optimal. Red Hat CTO Chris Wright reaches the same conclusion from a vendor's side: most community improvement happens by modifying weights or through [[FineTuning|fine-tuning]], and data's link to the trained weights is "tenuous and indirect." Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller of Open Future add a supply argument — open resources lack the volume to train large foundation models, so nearly every open effort depends on web-scraped data.

**Position B — training data as a requirement**: The [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|Free Software Foundation]] (FSF) holds that a machine-learning application is not free unless its training data and processing scripts respect the four freedoms. AWS open source strategists Tom Callaway and julia ferraioli press the same line. Callaway calls a data-optional definition a "loophole" that devalues "open source" in every other context, and ferraioli argues that without data users can only use a system and build on it by fine-tuning. OSI co-founder Bruce Perens holds that the training data is the source code, so the original Open Source Definition could cover it, and judges the OSAID "less than Open Source." A Debian General Resolution proposed by Mo Zhou would carry the position into distribution policy, beyond the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]]'s own forum. It would declare models released without their original training data non-free under the Debian Free Software Guidelines (DFSG).

**Position C — keep the floor, change the name**: Tarkowski and Keller accept the data-information floor as the right call for developers. They argue the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]] should have named it after [[OpenWeights|open weights]] and reserved "open source" for systems with open data. They frame the choice as "a stronger but narrower standard" against "a weaker but broader" one, and say a tiered scheme like the [[ModelOpennessFramework|Model Openness Framework]] would have resolved it.

## Representative Evidence

- [[osi-readies-controversial-osaid#Key Quotes|LWN's pre-vote survey]] (2024-10-25, the oldest source on this axis) — Joe Brockmeier sets the OSI's own voice beside its critics: the 0.0.9 announcement calls data "a benefit, not a requirement," Maffulli answers "We have not compromised on our principles," and the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|FSF]] states "we cannot say a ML application 'is free' unless all its training data" respects the four freedoms.
- [[techcrunch-osaid-official-definition#Key Quotes|TechCrunch on the 1.0 release]] — Kyle Wiggers reports the release requirement that a model be documented well enough to be "substantially" recreated, and quotes Lightning AI CTO Luca Antiga: "By neglecting to deal with licensing of training data, the OSI is leaving a gaping hole."
- [[open-future-osaid-step-forward#Key Quotes|Open Future's assessment]] — Tarkowski and Keller call training datasets "the primary component that can be understood as the source of AI systems," yet judge the absence of an open-data requirement "the correct choice" from developers' perspective.
- [[red-hat-open-source-ai-point-of-view#Key Quotes|Red Hat's point of view]] — Chris Wright sets Red Hat's minimum threshold as "open source-licensed model weights combined with open source software components" and writes that "Training data alone does not fit this role."
- [[case-against-osaid#Key Quotes|The New Stack's round-up]] — David Cassel collects the critics and reports Perens's view that the training data is the source code. Against the OSI FAQ's forecast that an open-data requirement would leave open source AI a "tiny" niche, he lists open-data releases: a 2-trillion-token dataset from Pleias, LLMs with open training data from Ai2, and "fully open 1 billion parameter language models" from AMD.
- [[debian-ai-models-dfsg#Key Quotes|LWN on the Debian resolution]] (2025-04-25) — Mo Zhou's text holds that models "without original training data or program" are not DFSG-compliant, and Gunnar Wolf argues Debian "cannot magically extend DFSG-freeness to a binary we have no way to recreate."

## Derived Tensions & Generational Readings

Both camps concede ground to the other, and those concessions shape the dispute more than the headline positions do. All three voices that raise medical data — the OSI FAQ, Callaway and the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|FSF]] — concede it as the case where withholding training data is defensible; they differ on the label. The FAQ keeps such systems inside open source, the FSF calls them nonfree but possibly "ethically excusable" to use, and Callaway would accept "less Open Source AI systems at first" rather than compromise. Callaway also grants the other reasons to withhold data — its monetary value, redistribution bans, copyright risk. On the other side, Red Hat calls its weights-first threshold "a starting point of open source AI, not the final destination." [[Mozilla]], an endorser, acknowledges in [[mozilla-celebrates-osaid|its endorsement post]] that some disagree with the training-data treatment and that the definition will need refinement. Both camps also claim to fight [[OpenWashing|open-washing]]. The canonical [[osi-open-source-ai-definition|OSAID text]] names combating it as the definition's motivation, while Open Future reports that Open Data advocates see the missing data requirement as itself "a form of open washing."

The strict camp is not one position either. "Open data" can mean access to the dataset, a distributable license for it, or reproducible training from it, and different critics stress different ones. Antiga's objection is about [[ModelLicensing|licensing]] of the data, not disclosure. In Debian, Thorsten Glaser's counter-proposal adds that models be trained only on "legally obtained and used works" and be rebuilt reproducibly — a stance he himself calls "a hard anti-AI stance (with select exceptions)." Zhou's proposal also lists as a downside that almost no useful AI models could enter Debian's main archive, which is the practical cost the A camp predicts.

The dispute has also moved venues over time. It began inside the OSI's co-design process: the 0.0.9 draft of August 2024 already named training data "one of the most hotly debated parts." Post-release criticism followed in late 2024. In February 2025 Red Hat set out its own minimum threshold, which it says is not a formal definition. In April 2025 the question reached Debian's governance. Along the way the corpus records an industry trend toward less data transparency. Open Future ties it to competition and copyright liability, and [[Meta]] told TechCrunch it takes a "cautious approach" to sharing model details, including details about its training data. The generational reading runs through the history of the term. Perens holds that the inherited definition, with data treated as source code, applies to AI unchanged. Maffulli and RedMonk's Stephen O'Grady, from opposite conclusions, agree it cannot simply be translated to AI.

A note on the evidence grade: nearly every claim here is `[analysis]`-grade argument by a named claimant, with `[fact]`-grade material limited to what each definition or proposal requires. The corpus holds no measurement of how often data access actually changes what modifiers can do, so each camp's central premise rests on attribution to practitioners rather than on data. Only one source rebuts the other side head-on: Red Hat's point of view rejects the premise, reported in [[case-against-osaid|the round-up of critics]] from Perens, that training data is the equivalent of a model's source code.

## Interpretive Direction

In the short term, what [[OpenSourceAI|open source AI]] requires of data looks set to be settled by different institutions, not within one. The OSAID's data-information floor stands as the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]]'s reference, while the [[FreeSoftwareFoundation|FSF]]'s pending criteria and the Debian resolution would set a data-required bar for free software. Open Future's "stronger but narrower" against "weaker but broader" framing describes the trade-off without choosing a winner, and the evidence here does not settle which side of it the community will take. The open-data releases in the round-up answer the objection, which one observer calls central, that there is "no good example of an open data LLM." At 1 billion parameters, though, AMD's models do not reach the scale objections the other side actually makes: Open Future's, that open resources are too thin to train large foundation models, and the OSI FAQ's, that a data requirement would leave a "tiny" niche. Nor do the releases show data should be mandatory, and a companion analysis, [[open-source-ai-every-camp-standard|what clears every camp's bar]], finds no release documented as meeting both standards.

Points to monitor, one set per camp. On the data-required side, watch whether the Debian resolution passed — the corpus records Brockmeier's forecast that it was likely to, but no vote result as of 2026-09-24 — and whether the FSF publishes its free machine-learning criteria or the OSI's amendment committee revisits the data clause. On the data-information side, watch whether the EU AI Act's training-data transparency template takes the OSAID floor as its reference point, a role Open Future argues the OSAID can play for the template blueprint it drafted with Mozilla and several open source AI developers. The newest source on this axis dates from April 2025, so any of these may already have moved outside the corpus. The parallel dispute over the OSAID's binary form and its board-level legitimacy is followed in [[osaid-form-and-legitimacy|the binary-definition theme]].
