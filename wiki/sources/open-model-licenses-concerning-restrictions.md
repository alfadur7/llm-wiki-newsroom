## Summary

In a 2025-03-14 TechCrunch article, AI editor Kyle Wiggers reports that the custom licenses on "open" models from Google and [[Meta]] make commercial use legally risky. He cites Meta's Llama 3 license, which bars using its outputs to improve other models and requires a separate license above 700 million monthly active users. He also cites Google's Gemma license, which lets Google restrict usage remotely. Nick Vidal of the [[OpenSourceInitiative]], researcher Florian Brand, Gretel's Eric Tramel, and [[HuggingFace]]'s Yacine Jernite argue that these [[ModelLicensing|model licenses]] deter adoption, and Brand says they cannot reasonably be called open source. Wiggers notes that Llama has still been downloaded hundreds of millions of times.

## Key Claims

- [fact] Kyle Wiggers — reports that Google released Gemma 3, a family of open AI models, and that several developers said on X that its license makes commercial use risky
- [analysis] Kyle Wiggers — writes that [[Meta]] and other companies apply custom, non-standard licensing terms to their openly available models, and that these terms present legal challenges for companies
- [analysis] Kyle Wiggers — reports that some firms, especially smaller ones, worry that Google and others could "pull the rug" on their business by asserting the more onerous clauses
- [analysis] Nick Vidal — says the restrictive and inconsistent licensing of so-called "open" AI models is creating significant uncertainty, particularly for commercial adoption
- [analysis] Nick Vidal — says models marketed as open carry terms that impose legal and practical hurdles, which deter businesses from building them into products
- [fact] Kyle Wiggers — reports that AI startup Cohere has been clear that it intends to support scientific but not commercial work on top of its models
- [fact] Kyle Wiggers — reports that [[Meta]]'s license prohibits developers from using the "output or results" of Llama 3 models to improve any model besides Llama 3 or its derivative works
- [fact] Kyle Wiggers — reports that [[Meta]]'s license prevents companies with over 700 million monthly active users from deploying Llama models without first obtaining a special, additional license
- [fact] Kyle Wiggers — reports that Gemma's license grants Google the right to "restrict (remotely or otherwise) usage" that it believes violates its prohibited use policy or applicable laws
- [fact] Kyle Wiggers — reports that models based on Llama or Gemma must also follow the Llama or Gemma license, and that for Gemma this includes models trained on synthetic data generated by Gemma
- [analysis] Florian Brand — argues that licenses like Gemma's and Llama's "cannot reasonably be called 'open source'"
- [analysis] Florian Brand — says most companies keep a set of approved licenses such as Apache 2.0, so any custom license costs them trouble and money
- [forecast] Florian Brand — expects small companies without legal teams to stick to models with standard licenses
- [analysis] Florian Brand — observes that developers with custom licenses, such as Google, have not aggressively enforced their terms yet, but that the threat alone often deters adoption
- [analysis] Han-Chung Lee — agrees that custom licenses such as those on Gemma and Llama make the models "not usable" in many commercial scenarios
- [analysis] Eric Tramel — says model-specific licenses carve out model derivatives and [[Distillation|distillation]], which raises concern about clawbacks
- [analysis] Eric Tramel — says deployers most fear that a model developer could release open models, watch which business cases succeed, and then force its way into those markets through extortion or lawfare
- [forecast] Eric Tramel — expects businesses to stick with possibly weaker Apache 2.0 models because Gemma 3's license structure keeps the market from adopting it
- [fact] Kyle Wiggers — reports that Llama has been downloaded hundreds of millions of times and built into products from major corporations, including Spotify
- [analysis] Yacine Jernite — argues that models such as Llama could be even more successful if they were permissively licensed, and calls on providers like Google to move to open license frameworks
- [analysis] Yacine Jernite — says that because many of the licenses' underlying assumptions are untested in court, the terms serve mainly as a declaration of intent from the companies
- [analysis] Nick Vidal — calls for the AI industry to align with established open source principles instead of redefining "open" to suit corporate interests

## Key Quotes

> "The restrictive and inconsistent licensing of so-called 'open' AI models is creating significant uncertainty, particularly for commercial adoption. While these models are marketed as open, the actual terms impose various legal and practical hurdles that deter businesses from integrating them into their products or services." — Nick Vidal, head of community at the Open Source Initiative

> "Most companies have a set of approved licenses, such as Apache 2.0, so any custom license is a lot of trouble and money. Small companies without legal teams or money for lawyers will stick to models with standard licenses." — Florian Brand, AI researcher at Trier University

> "These restrictions have an impact on the AI ecosystem — even on AI researchers like me." — Florian Brand, AI researcher at Trier University

> "Model-specific licenses make specific carve-outs for model derivatives and distillation, which causes concern about clawbacks." — Eric Tramel, staff applied scientist at Gretel

> "A model foundry can put out [open] models, wait to see what business cases develop using those models, and then strong-arm their way into successful verticals by either extortion or lawfare." — Eric Tramel, staff applied scientist at Gretel

> "Given the lack of consensus on these terms and the fact that many of the underlying assumptions haven't yet been tested in courts, it all serves primarily as a declaration of intent from those actors." — Yacine Jernite, head of machine learning and society at Hugging Face

> "The current landscape of AI model licensing is riddled with confusion, restrictive terms, and misleading claims of openness. Instead of redefining 'open' to suit corporate interests, the AI industry should align with established open source principles to create a truly open ecosystem." — Nick Vidal, head of community at the Open Source Initiative
