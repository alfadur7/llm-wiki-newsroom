## Summary

In an October 25, 2024 opinion column for The Register, Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols argues that [[Meta]]'s Llama 3 is not open source, because its license's clauses on litigation and branding fail the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]]. He calls the wider practice [[OpenWashing|open washing]], a term he traces to Michelle Thorne in 2009, and cites a Radboud University survey finding that most models billed as open are not. He names the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]]'s exemptions for open-source models as a strong incentive to open-wash, and he quotes Chainguard CEO Dan Lorenc warning that a diluted definition would push developers back into license-by-license legal review. The column carries no response from Meta; it is an opinion piece, and external assessment of its claims is outside the raw's scope.

## Key Claims

- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — reports that Mark Zuckerberg describes [[Meta]]'s Llama 3 as open source
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — argues that Llama 3's license, with its clauses on litigation and branding, fails the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]] on several grounds
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — defines [[OpenWashing|open washing]] as presenting products, services, or processes as "open" when they are not open in the spirit of transparency, access to information, participation, and knowledge sharing
- [fact] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — states that the term open washing, modeled on "greenwashing," was coined in 2009 by Michelle Thorne, an internet and climate policy scholar
- [fact] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — reports that Radboud University researchers Andreas Liesenfeld and Mark Dingemanse surveyed 45 text and text-to-image models that claim to be open
- [analysis] Andreas Liesenfeld and Mark Dingemanse — found that a handful of lesser-known LLMs, such as AllenAI's OLMo and the BigScience Workshop and [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]] model BloomZ, could be considered open
- [analysis] Andreas Liesenfeld and Mark Dingemanse — found that most models claiming to be open are not, including the big-name models from Google, [[Meta]], and Microsoft
- [fact] Steve Ballmer — proclaimed in 2001 that "Linux is a cancer," on the grounds that using open source software forces the rest of a company's software to be open source
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — argues that open washing lets companies capitalize on the positive perception of open source without committing to open practices
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — argues that some corporations use open washing to shield their models and practices from scientific and regulatory scrutiny
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — argues that the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]]'s exemptions for "open source" models create a powerful incentive to open-wash, because qualifying models face less restrictive requirements
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — argues that qualifying as open under the EU AI Act would cut the cost of meeting regulatory requirements and of cleaning datasets of copyright and other IP issues
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — states that the EU has no clear definition of open source AI and that no one else has one yet either
- [forecast] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — expects the [[OpenSourceInitiative|Open Source Initiative]] to release its [[OpenSourceAI|open source AI definition]] within days
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — argues that the current open-washing licenses fail by anyone's definition other than their creators'
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — states that IBM's Granite 3.0 LLMs are open source under the Apache 2 license
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — notes that the [[OpenSourceInitiative|OSI]] is a nonprofit that publishes guidelines, not a government or regulatory body
- [fact] Dan Lorenc — said in a keynote at the Secure Open Source Software (SOSS) Fusion Conference in Atlanta that no one can force anyone to use the OSI's definitions
- [fact] Dan Lorenc — said that lawyers trust the OSI's definition, which is why it appears in the procurement contracts of large companies worldwide
- [forecast] Dan Lorenc — warned that without a trusted definition, developers would need a legal review every time they adopt a new library
- [forecast] Dan Lorenc — warned that companies would become afraid to publish source code if its liabilities were unclear
- [forecast] Dan Lorenc — warned that eroding the definition could stop projects working, leave security bugs unfixed, and make maintenance harder
- [analysis] Dan Lorenc — called on everyone who uses open source to act together to preserve and defend the definition of open source
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — argues that [[ModelLicensing|license]] carve-outs such as "open except for someone who might compete with us" or "open except for someone who might deploy the code on a cloud" invite trouble
- [forecast] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — predicts that open washing will harm legal, business, and development work for everyone, including the companies that support it
- [analysis] Steven J. Vaughan-Nichols — argues that almost all of these companies' AI work is ultimately built on open source

## Key Quotes

> "Linux is a cancer." — Steve Ballmer, 2001

> "The way the license is written, if you use any open source software, you have to make the rest of your software open source." — Steve Ballmer, 2001

> "Fortunately, many people, particularly lawyers, believe in this definition. They trust the work that the OSI does, and they trust and understand the protections that companies are granted when they use these licenses when they meet the open source criteria. That's why we see it showing up in procurement contracts of big companies all over the world." — Dan Lorenc, Chainguard CEO

> "Developers are going to go to legal reviews every time you want to use a new library. Companies are going to be scared to publish things on the internet if they're not clear about the liabilities they're encountering when that source code becomes public." — Dan Lorenc, Chainguard CEO

> "You might think this is only a big company problem, but it's not. It's a shared problem. Everybody who uses open source is going to be affected by this. It could cause entire projects to stop working. Security bugs aren't going to get fixed. Maintenance is going to get a lot harder. We must act together to preserve and defend the definition of open source. Otherwise, the lawyers are going to have to come back. No one wants the lawyers to come back." — Dan Lorenc, Chainguard CEO
