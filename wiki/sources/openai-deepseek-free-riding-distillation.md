## Summary

A Rest of World article by Kinling Lo, dated 2026-02-13, reports that [[OpenAI]] told the U.S. House Select Committee on China in a 2026-02-12 memo that [[DeepSeek]]'s next model should be understood as part of an effort to "free-ride" on U.S. frontier labs. According to the memo, accounts tied to DeepSeek employees circumvented OpenAI's access restrictions through obfuscated third-party routers and wrote code to pull model outputs for [[Distillation|distillation]]. The article places the memo against expectations of a DeepSeek launch around the Lunar New Year, a year after R1, and against China's turn to [[OpenWeights|open-weight]] models. Two analysts question the accusation: RAND's Austin Horng-En Wang suggests it aims to keep chips from Chinese firms, and Counterpoint's Neil Shah says the whole industry has evolved through "recursive learning." The raw carries no DeepSeek response to the accusation; it states only that DeepSeek had not confirmed any new launch.

- [analysis] [[OpenAI]] — states in the memo its belief that DeepSeek also uses third-party routers to access frontier models from other U.S. labs [[openai-deepseek-free-riding-distillation#Key Quotes]]
- [fact] [[OpenAI]] — says it does not permit its outputs to be used to create "imitation frontier AI models" that replicate its capabilities
- [fact] Rest of World — reports that DeepSeek had not confirmed any new launches when the memo was sent
- [analysis] Rest of World — suggests OpenAI's concerns may stem from expectations that DeepSeek could make a major announcement during the Lunar New Year celebrations
- [fact] Rest of World — reports that DeepSeek released its R1 model during the previous year's Lunar New Year period, claiming performance comparable to top U.S. models despite training with far fewer advanced chips
- [analysis] Rest of World — reports that the R1 launch reignited debate in Washington over whether U.S. export controls on advanced semiconductors were sufficient to preserve America's AI lead
- [analysis] Rest of World — describes distillation as a common method in which a smaller model is trained on the outputs of a more powerful one to replicate its capabilities
- [fact] Rest of World — reports that OpenAI investigated whether DeepSeek had distilled its data right after R1 launched in January of the previous year
- [analysis] Austin Horng-En Wang — questions why OpenAI escalated now, as Chinese companies and policymakers have openly pushed for an open-source AI ecosystem since DeepSeek's breakthrough
- [analysis] Austin Horng-En Wang — suggests the accusation may aim to keep DeepSeek and other Chinese companies from acquiring more chips, so U.S. models keep their lead [[openai-deepseek-free-riding-distillation#Key Quotes]]
- [analysis] Rest of World — reports that R1 helped spur China's embrace of open-weight models that developers worldwide can download, modify, and deploy
- [analysis] Rest of World — contrasts that approach with the closed systems most U.S. tech giants favor, which tightly control access to their models, data, and architecture
- [fact] Rest of World — reports that Chinese tech giants and AI startups rushed to release their latest open models in the month before the article, ahead of DeepSeek
- [analysis] Neil Shah — says the conflict reflects larger tensions in global AI development and a constant fight over model knowledge, capability, accuracy, and efficiency
- [analysis] Neil Shah — argues that no model is an island and that newer entrants often follow the same routes of distillation and optimization [[openai-deepseek-free-riding-distillation#Key Quotes]]

## Key Quotes

> "DeepSeek's next model (whatever its form) should be understood in the context of its ongoing efforts to free-ride on the capabilities developed by OpenAI and other US frontier labs." — [[OpenAI]], memo to the U.S. House Select Committee on China

> "We have observed accounts associated with DeepSeek employees developing methods to circumvent OpenAI's access restrictions and access models through obfuscated third-party routers and other ways that mask their source. We also know that DeepSeek employees developed code to access U.S. AI models and obtain outputs for distillation in programmatic ways. We believe that DeepSeek also uses third-party routers to access frontier models from other U.S. labs." — [[OpenAI]], memo to the U.S. House Select Committee on China

> "In this high-stakes AI arms race, there is a constant fight on the knowledge, capability, accuracy, and efficiency of the models." — Neil Shah, vice president of research at Counterpoint Research

> "One possible reason for the accusation is to prevent DeepSeek and China companies from acquiring more chips to distill the U.S. model, so that the U.S. models can keep their leading position." — Austin Horng-En Wang, associate political scientist at RAND Corporation

> "The reality is none of the models is an island and the entire industry has mostly evolved based on recursive learning. The newer entrants are in many instances going through the same routes of 'distillation' and 'optimization.'" — Neil Shah, vice president of research at Counterpoint Research
