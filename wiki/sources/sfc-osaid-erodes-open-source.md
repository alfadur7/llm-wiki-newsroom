## Summary

In a Software Freedom Conservancy blog post dated 2024-10-31, Bradley M. Kühn argues that the [[OpenSourceInitiative]]'s newly official Open Source AI Definition (OSAID) 1.0 departs from what most software freedom advocates mean by "open source." His central objection is that the definition does not require public reproducibility, because it places too few licensing and disclosure requirements on [[TrainingData|training sets]]. He contrasts the OSAID's rapid drafting with the decades of public discussion behind the 1999 Open Source Definition, and announces a single-issue run for the OSI Board to have the OSAID republished as "recommendations." OSI's position appears only as Kühn paraphrases it; the post carries no OSI response.

## Key Claims

- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — reports that the [[OpenSourceInitiative]] made the OSAID official with its 1.0 release in the week of his 2024-10-31 post
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — argues the OSAID fails to require public reproducibility of how AI systems are built, because it sets insufficient licensing and disclosure requirements on [[TrainingData|training sets]]
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — attributes OSI's refusal to add a training-data requirement to its decision that "there was no point in publishing a definition that no existing AI system could currently meet"
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — argues that this compromise amplified the role of stakeholders who would financially benefit from OSI retroactively declaring their systems open source
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — argues OSI should have issued the document as "recommendations" or "guidelines" instead of a "definition" carrying authority equivalent to the Open Source Definition
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — states that Microsoft's GitHub was the "Thought Leader" for the AI "deep dive" OSI announced when it turned its attention to AI
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — argues OSI responded too rapidly to industry hype, which made it an easy target for regulatory capture
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — states the original Open Source Definition was first published in February 1999, at least twelve years after widespread industry adoption of FOSS programs such as the GNU C Compiler and BSD
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — states the Open Source Definition was based in large part on the independently developed Debian Free Software Guidelines (DFSG), which balanced OSI's authority
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — states that OSI failed to pursue trademark protection for "open source" in its early days
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — argues that without a trademark OSI cannot stop Mark Zuckerberg from applying the "open source" label to [[Meta]]'s Llama, which undercuts OSI's claim that it had to move quickly to stop companies co-opting the term
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — argues that defining the field from the tiny 2024 sample of LLM-backed systems built in a bias toward accepting first movers as legitimate, so the OSAID endorses "demonstrably bad" systems as open source by definition
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — argues OSI disenfranchised users and content creators, asking where painters, novelists, actors, playwrights, musicians, and poets were in the drafting process
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — argues the line between data and code is crossed so easily in these systems that the adage "data is separate and can be proprietary" no longer holds
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — states he can no longer say that anything OSI calls "open source" gives users all the rights and freedoms they deserve, unless or until OSI revokes the OSAID
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — states that OSI plans only incremental updates to the OSAID rather than revoking it
- [forecast] Bradley M. Kühn — expects that once entities rely on OSAID 1.0, OSI will find it nearly impossible to later declare those systems no longer open source, leaving its key problems in place
- [analysis] Bradley M. Kühn — calls publicly available [[TrainingData|training sets]] under Free Software licenses the ideal and preferred form for modification, while saying he does not yet know whether they are the only way to respect user rights
- [forecast] Bradley M. Kühn — expects it will take at least a decade to find where the bright line of acceptable behavior lies for these AI systems
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — announced he will run for the OSI Board of Directors on a single-issue platform to repeal the OSAID and republish it as recommendations
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — called on other software rights activists to run with him as a ticket at the next OSI Board election
- [fact] Bradley M. Kühn — states that OSI's by-laws allow the current Board to reject election results, so those results are not binding

## Key Quotes

> "Until today, I was always able to say: “I believe that anything the OSI calls ‘open source’ gives you all the rights and freedoms that you deserve”. I now cannot say that again unless/until the OSI revokes the OSAID." — Bradley M. Kühn, Software Freedom Conservancy

> "By making this definition official too soon, OSI has endorsed demonstrably bad LLM-backed generative AI systems as “open source” by definition!" — Bradley M. Kühn, Software Freedom Conservancy

> "The humble act now is to admit that it was just too soon to publish a “definition” and rebrand these the OSAID 1.0 as “current recommendations”." — Bradley M. Kühn, Software Freedom Conservancy

> "[M]y role is to be on the bottom of things. … I try to digest … knowledge into a form that is accessible to people who don't have time for such study" — Donald E. Knuth, computer scientist (quoted by Kühn)
