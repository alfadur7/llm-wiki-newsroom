## Summary

This MIT Technology Review feature, dated 2024-03-25 by its URL, reports on the Open Source Initiative's early effort to define open-source AI through a group of about 70 participants. OSI executive director Stefano Maffulli says the freedoms are settled but the components needed to exercise them are not, and training data is the biggest sticking point. Participants split on data: Roman Shaposhnik sees a compromise in sharing open corpora, while Zuzanna Warso of Open Future ties withheld data to open-washing and the concentration of power. Sarah Myers West, Danish Contractor and Luis Villa widen the debate to the goals of openness, use-restricting licenses such as RAIL, and the risk that Meta will define "open" if the community does not.

## Key Claims

- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — assembled a group of about 70 researchers, lawyers, policymakers, activists and representatives of Meta, Google and Amazon to write a working definition of open-source AI
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — was founded in 1998 and is the custodian of the [[OpenSourceDefinition|Open Source Definition]]
- [analysis] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — says there is broad agreement on overarching principles but that the difficulty lies in the details
- [fact] [[Meta]] — released Llama 2 freely in July 2023 and referred to it as open source
- [fact] [[Meta]] — said through associate general counsel Jonathan Torres that it supports the OSI's effort to define open-source AI and will keep participating in the process
- [fact] [[OpenAI]] — said through a spokesperson that it open-sources powerful models only after weighing benefits and risks, including misuse and acceleration
- [fact] Google — released its Gemma models in February 2024 as freely accessible and described them as "open" rather than "open source"
- [analysis] MIT Technology Review — reports that the Llama 2 and Gemma licenses restrict what users can do, while the Open Source Definition outlaws restrictions based on use cases
- [analysis] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — explains that key open-source concepts do not translate neatly from software to AI because a model has many more ingredients than source code
- [analysis] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — says the OSI has identified the basic freedoms it wants but that the mechanics of exercising them remain unclear
- [analysis] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — argues that a widely adopted definition would bring lower compliance costs, less friction and shared understanding
- [analysis] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — identifies [[TrainingData|training data]] as the biggest sticking point, since major AI companies release pretrained models without their datasets
- [analysis] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — reports that other participants hold a description of the data is often enough, because models can be adapted through [[FineTuning|fine-tuning]] without retraining
- [analysis] Roman Shaposhnik — cites Llama 2 as a pretrained-only release around which a developer ecosystem has grown, so it "must" be called something
- [analysis] Zuzanna Warso — argues that restricting access to training data is not in the spirit of open source and that openness should extend to data
- [analysis] Zuzanna Warso — explains that high-quality training data is a competitive advantage that larger firms want to keep
- [analysis] Zuzanna Warso — says that calling a release "open source" can be an easy PR win through [[OpenWashing|open-washing]]
- [fact] Harvard Business School economists — found that open-source software has saved companies almost $9 trillion in development costs
- [fact] [[MarkZuckerberg|Mark Zuckerberg]] — said in earnings calls that open-source software often becomes an industry standard, which makes it easier to integrate new innovations into Meta's products
- [analysis] Zuzanna Warso — points to the [[EUAIAct|EU AI Act]] exempting certain open-source projects from some of its stricter requirements as a regulatory benefit of the label
- [analysis] Zuzanna Warso — argues that sharing pretrained models while withholding data lets companies "have their cake and eat it too"
- [analysis] Roman Shaposhnik — proposes that companies share the open resources, such as Wikipedia and Common Crawl, used to train their models so others can recreate a reasonable approximation
- [analysis] Aviya Skowron — explains that unclear copyright status of scraped art and writing makes developers wary of being open about their data
- [analysis] Stefano Zacchiroli — holds that a full description of a model's training data is the bare minimum for calling it open source, while recognizing stricter definitions may lack broad appeal
- [analysis] Sarah Myers West — argues that the data and compute needed for cutting-edge AI create structural barriers for smaller players however open models are
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — lists autonomy and transparency as key benefits in its draft definition and places "ethical, trustworthy, or responsible" AI in an "out of scope issues" section
- [analysis] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — says the open-source community has historically avoided debates about what software should be used for
- [analysis] Zuzanna Warso — argues that treating ethics as "out of scope" is a myth kept up to hold the open-source coalition together
- [fact] Danish Contractor — co-created the Responsible AI Licenses (RAIL) in 2022, which resemble open-source licenses but can restrict specific use cases
- [fact] Danish Contractor — found with colleagues that 28% of models on [[HuggingFace|Hugging Face]]'s platform use RAIL
- [fact] Google — attached terms of use to Gemma listing prohibited "harmful" use cases, which it tied to its commitment to developing AI responsibly
- [fact] [[Ai2|Allen Institute for AI]] — developed ImpACT Licenses that restrict redistribution of models and data based on their potential risks
- [analysis] Luis Villa — warns that a proliferation of mutually incompatible "open-ish" [[ModelLicensing|licenses]] could negate the frictionless collaboration behind open source's success
- [forecast] Luis Villa — expects industry to decide for itself what "open" means unless the community coalesces quickly around a single standard

## Key Quotes

> "We support the OSI's effort to define open-source AI and look forward to continuing to participate in their process for the benefit of the open source community across the world." — Jonathan Torres, Meta associate general counsel for AI, open source and licensing

> "We only open-source powerful AI models once we have carefully weighed the benefits and risks, including misuse and acceleration." — [[OpenAI]] spokesperson

> "We have identified what basic freedoms or basic rights we want to be able to exercise. The mechanics of how to exercise those rights are not clear." — [[StefanoMaffulli]], OSI executive director

> "Having [a definition] that is respected and adopted by a large chunk of the industry provides clarity. And with clarity comes lower costs for compliance, less friction, shared understanding." — [[StefanoMaffulli]], OSI executive director

> "People are using it in all sorts of projects. There's a whole ecosystem around it. We therefore must call it something. Is it half-open? Is it ajar?" — Roman Shaposhnik, Ainekko CEO and Apache Software Foundation VP of legal affairs

> "It's a crucial component of this whole process. If we care about openness, we should also care about the openness of the data." — Zuzanna Warso, Open Future director of research

> "We see openness as one of the tools to challenge the concentration of power. If the definition is supposed to help in challenging these concentrations of power, then the question of data becomes even more important." — Zuzanna Warso, Open Future director of research

> "open-source software often becomes an industry standard, and when companies standardize on building with our stack, that then becomes easier to integrate new innovations into our products." — [[MarkZuckerberg|Mark Zuckerberg]], Meta CEO

> "Are you just following where the market is going so that they don't essentially co-opt the term 'open-source AI,' or are you trying to pull the market toward being more open and providing more freedoms to the users?" — Stefano Zacchiroli, Polytechnic Institute of Paris

> "Is it safety, is it the ability to conduct academic research, is it trying to foster greater competition? We need to be way more precise about what the goal is, and then how opening up a system changes the pursuit of that goal." — Sarah Myers West, AI Now Institute co-executive director

> "It's not our job." — [[StefanoMaffulli]], OSI executive director

> "I think people realize it's not real [the myth], but we need this to move forward." — Zuzanna Warso, Open Future director of research

> "As a researcher, I would hate for my stuff to be used in ways that would be detrimental." — Danish Contractor, RAIL co-creator

> "They're going to fill that vacuum. Mark Zuckerberg is going to tell us all what he thinks 'open' means, and he has a very big megaphone." — Luis Villa, Tidelift cofounder
