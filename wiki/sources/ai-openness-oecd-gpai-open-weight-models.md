## Summary

A 2025-08-28 post on the OECD.AI "AI Wonk" blog by OECD staff Luis Aranda and Karine Perset summarizes the Global Partnership on AI (GPAI) report *AI Openness: A Primer for Policymakers*, framing it against [[OpenAI]]'s August 2025 release of the [[welcome-gpt-oss-openai|GPT-OSS]] open-weight models. According to the report, "open-source AI" is an imprecise term because code, documentation and data can each be opened to different degrees, so openness is a spectrum; the report therefore narrows its scope to [[OpenWeights|open-weight]] foundation models. It lists five benefits and five risks of releasing weights, and recommends a marginal analysis of what releasing weights adds over keeping a model closed, embedded in broader risk management. The post carries a disclaimer that its views are the authors', not the OECD's or GPAI's; the report's full text is outside the raw's scope.

## Key Claims

- [fact] Luis Aranda and Karine Perset — report that OpenAI announced GPT-OSS in August 2025, a family of open-weight models that give public access to the trained parameters of a frontier-level AI system
- [analysis] Luis Aranda and Karine Perset — describe the reaction to GPT-OSS as split, with some hailing it as a win for transparency and innovation and others warning it could accelerate malicious uses of advanced AI
- [analysis] Luis Aranda and Karine Perset — call the GPT-OSS release emblematic because it marks a significant shift for a company that until recently championed a closed-model approach
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — explain that terms such as "open-source AI" are imprecise because AI models, unlike traditional software, consist of code, documentation and data layers
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — hold that each layer can be opened or restricted to varying degrees, so AI openness is a spectrum rather than a binary
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — note that a model may release its inference code openly while keeping its weights closed, or share weights with limited documentation or gated licences
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — assess that more permissive licences can encourage innovation through widespread experimentation but may offer fewer safeguards against misuse
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — assess that more restrictive licences can incentivise model development and investment but might limit broader collaboration
- [fact] OECD/GPAI — focus the primer on open-weight models, for practical reasons of a clearer, more actionable discussion
- [fact] OECD/GPAI — find that more than half of all commercially available foundation models in 2025 are open-weight, citing OECD.AI's internal AIKoD experimental database
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — identify actors in the United States, China and France as leading open-weight development, reflecting global competition and national strategies for technological sovereignty
- [fact] OECD.AI — chart the largest open-weight model offerings as coming from providers in the United States, the Netherlands and Singapore, using AIKoD data last updated 2025-04-30
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — observe that the average performance of open-weight models has improved rapidly since early 2024
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — argue that open-weight models lower barriers to entry, letting start-ups, researchers and smaller governments save on compute and fostering a more competitive market
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — argue that open weights let independent experts evaluate risks, biases and vulnerabilities, in line with the OECD AI Principle of transparency and explainability
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — argue that universities and training programmes benefit when students can [[FineTuning|fine-tune]] and experiment with advanced systems
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — argue that open weights let companies and governments deploy AI locally, including in sectors such as healthcare and national security where data is too sensitive to share
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — argue that open weights let developers adapt models to less common regions and cultural contexts
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — warn that once weights are released, control over the model's use is effectively lost and safeguards can be circumvented
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — warn that open-weight models can be misused for automated cyberattacks or for creating intimate images without consent
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — warn that models may memorise sensitive or copyrighted material that opening them could expose without consent
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — warn that modified versions of open-weight models can circulate widely, making risks harder to track and standards harder to enforce
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — warn that releasing one model's weights can expose weaknesses in other models and make them easier to trick into harmful output
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — judge risks such as misuse in biological or chemical fields possible but uncertain because evidence is limited
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — propose marginal analysis, asking what additional risks or benefits arise when weights are shared compared with keeping the model closed or using tools such as search engines
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — reason that marginal risks of openness may be small when a model's capabilities are already widely accessible through closed commercial systems
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — caution that repeatedly using more permissive baselines can slowly raise tolerance for harm, a "boiling frog" effect
- [analysis] OECD/GPAI — recommend that marginal assessments be embedded in a broader risk-management strategy rather than conducted in isolation
- [analysis] Luis Aranda and Karine Perset — call the primer essential reading for governments, researchers and industry leaders that offers a lens for decision-making without dictating a single solution

## Key Quotes

> "Once model weights are released, control over how the model is used is effectively lost, and safeguards can be circumvented." — Luis Aranda and Karine Perset, OECD, summarizing the OECD/GPAI report

> "Repeatedly using more permissive baselines can slowly raise tolerance for harm (the 'boiling frog' effect)." — Luis Aranda and Karine Perset, OECD, summarizing the OECD/GPAI report

> "The goal is not openness for its own sake, nor secrecy for its own sake, but trustworthy AI that benefits us all." — Luis Aranda and Karine Perset, OECD
