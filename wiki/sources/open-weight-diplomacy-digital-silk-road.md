## Summary

In a Lawfare essay, Fordham Law School associate professor Chinmayi Sharma argues that the U.S. debate over Chinese [[OpenWeights|open-weight]] models asks the wrong question. Restriction hawks want to ban the weight file and openness advocates want to bless it. In her account, the larger contest is over whose models become the base layer of other countries' work. She reads China's AI buildout as a rerun of Huawei's telecom playbook: give the weights away, sell the stack around them, and own the dependency that follows. Her evidence runs from Huawei's credit-financed network sales and ZTE's 2006 Ethiopia deal to [[MoonshotAI|Moonshot AI]]'s Kimi K3 license, Alibaba's Qwen-to-cloud funnel and [[DeepSeek]]'s chip-oriented data format. She concludes that the analogy fails at the weight-file layer but holds at the buildout layer, and that the United States should offer open models with accessible compute and financing attached. The piece is a single-author essay; the Chinese companies and governments it describes are not quoted responding to it, and external assessment of its thesis is outside the raw's scope. The raw carries no publication date.

## Key Claims

- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Moonshot AI announced Kimi K3 on July 16 and released its weights on July 27
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that independent measures place Kimi K3 within one generation of U.S. frontier models
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that the Trump administration is reportedly weighing Entity List designations for Moonshot AI and an executive order making U.S. companies liable for hosting Chinese models
- [fact] Scott Bessent — said, as Treasury Secretary, that sanctions are "on the table"
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Nvidia, Microsoft, [[Meta]] and over 230 other tech companies and organizations signed an industry letter warning the U.S. against broad restrictions on open models
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that the open-weight debate has been conducted mainly in the register of existential risk and near-term misuse, which she calls real but not the largest thing at stake
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that the more consequential contest is over which countries' models become the base layers of everyone else's work
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that seven senators urged the Commerce Department to scrutinize Chinese models by invoking the program that removed Huawei equipment from American networks
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that December's annual defense budget bill ordered [[DeepSeek]]'s models purged from Pentagon systems within 30 days
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that China financed Huawei's customers through state credit lines at prices that bought structural leverage, not only market share
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — states that there is no publicly confirmed case of a Huawei backdoor exploited for state espionage, and that the dependency itself was the power
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Congress first appropriated $1.9 billion to remove Chinese equipment from rural U.S. networks, then had to find another $3 billion as approved costs rose to $4.98 billion
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that in 2006 ZTE provided Ethiopia $1.5 billion in state credit to build a national telecom network with no economic-liberalization conditions attached
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Ethiopia's mobile subscriptions grew more than tenfold in the five years after the ZTE deal
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that Ethiopia later had to split its follow-on contract between ZTE and Huawei to restore its own bargaining power
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that open weights play the role subsidized equipment once did, a nearly free entry point that is useless without a more expensive surrounding stack
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that open-weight models are not inherently sovereignty-enhancing
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — grants that a downloaded weight file, unlike a Huawei switch, can be copied, run on one's own machines and kept forever
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Southeast Asia's flagship regional models have deliberately mixed U.S. and Chinese base models as a hedge against dependency on either
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Malaysia's 2025 sovereign AI launch ran [[DeepSeek]] on Huawei chips and was retracted within a day amid U.S. pressure
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Huawei opened a cloud region in Egypt and launched an Arabic large language model with it
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Huawei pledged to train 150,000 people in sub-Saharan Africa and 300,000 in Pakistan
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that the state-backed lab Zhipu pitches "sovereign AI agents" directly to Belt and Road governments
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Beijing has issued a Global AI Governance Action Plan and set up a world AI cooperation organization headquartered in Shanghai
- [fact] [[DeepSeek]] — said in August 2025 that the data format of its new model "is designed for the next generation of domestically produced chips"
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that eight Chinese chipmakers had DeepSeek's latest model running on their silicon on day one
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — states that porting a Chinese model to U.S. chips still takes days, not years, so no model is yet a standard
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that Alibaba gives away its Qwen open-weight model as a funnel into Alibaba Cloud
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Alibaba Cloud's AI revenue helps justify roughly $17 billion a year in new infrastructure spending
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that the Kimi K3 license requires large hosting businesses to negotiate a separate agreement with Moonshot AI
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Moonshot AI's paid API revenue has reportedly tripled since Kimi K3's release
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — notes that [[Meta]]'s Llama license has a restrictive commercial clause of its own
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that Meta's open-model circuit ends in a private advertising business while China's ends in state-championed chips, state-financed data centers and a national strategy
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that adopters grow dependent through the next model release they lack, not through the weights they hold
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Beijing is reportedly weighing export controls on the weight files of its own labs' most advanced models
- [forecast] Chinmayi Sharma — expects Chinese labs to gain true customers if fine-tunes, toolchains and trained engineers accumulate on Chinese model families faster than adopters can migrate
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that model weights cannot be embargoed like equipment and that a true download ban may not be lawful
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — states that every restriction Congress has enacted runs through government purchasing, such as Pentagon systems, federal contracts and state devices
- [fact] Chinmayi Sharma — reports that Washington capped the developing world's compute with one rule in January and rescinded it by May
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that the U.S. is selling the closed dependence that sovereign AI initiatives are trying to escape
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — recommends open U.S. models worth adopting, offered with accessible compute and financing on terms a sovereign state can justify accepting
- [analysis] Chinmayi Sharma — argues that Nvidia is the closest thing the U.S. has to a welcome-phase competitor, because it strikes "sovereign AI" deals, even though its bid builds dependence on Nvidia
