## Summary

A VentureBeat report by Sharon Goldman, dated 2023-06-08, covers a letter that U.S. Senators Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley sent to [[Meta]] CEO Mark Zuckerberg about the March 2023 leak of Meta's LLaMA model. The senators questioned whether Meta's "unrestrained and permissive" distribution was appropriate, citing misuse risks such as spam, fraud, and malware. Five experts quoted in the piece defend open release: they call the letter a threat to open-source AI, a "misguided" attempt to limit access, or a form of "politics of intimidation," and some suspect proprietary competitors of steering the debate. The piece quotes no one who supports the letter. Meta declined to comment, and the senators' side appears only through the letter's own wording.

## Key Claims

- [fact] Sharon Goldman — reports that Senators Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley sent a letter to Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg on Tuesday questioning the March leak of Meta's LLaMA model
- [fact] Sharon Goldman — reports that the senators sent the letter on behalf of the Senate Subcommittee on Privacy, Technology & the Law, which Blumenthal chairs and where Hawley is ranking member
- [fact] Sharon Goldman — reports that OpenAI CEO Sam Altman testified before the same subcommittee three weeks earlier and agreed with calls for a new AI regulatory agency
- [fact] Richard Blumenthal — co-signed a letter expressing concern about LLaMA's "potential for its misuse in spam, fraud, malware, privacy violations, harassment, and other wrongdoing and harms"
- [fact] Josh Hawley — co-signed a letter stating that Meta released LLaMA in February for download by approved researchers "rather than centralizing and restricting access to the underlying data, software, and model"
- [fact] Richard Blumenthal — co-signed a letter stating that Meta's "unrestrained and permissive" distribution of LLaMA raises questions about when it is appropriate to openly release sophisticated AI models
- [fact] [[Meta]] — declined to comment on the letter
- [fact] Mark Zuckerberg — reaffirmed Meta's "commitment to an open science-based approach to AI research" at a company all-hands meeting the day of the report
- [analysis] Adam Thierer — argues that officials and proprietary competitors find open source an easy target because policymakers see it as harder to control and competitors see it as competition
- [analysis] William Falcon — calls the letter "super surprising" and says it "almost feels like OpenAI and Congress are working together now"
- [analysis] Steven Weber — argues that Microsoft, operating through OpenAI, is "running scared" of open-source AI the way it ran scared of Linux in the late 1990s
- [analysis] Christopher Manning — calls for the government and the AI community to develop regulations and norms that apply to all developers and users of large AI models
- [analysis] Christopher Manning — acknowledges that models like LLaMA can be used for disinformation or spam but supports their open-source release
- [fact] Christopher Manning — states that LLaMA models are smaller than the largest models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, which he puts at roughly 175 billion to 512 billion parameters
- [fact] Christopher Manning — states that the largest LLaMA model has 60 billion parameters, against 20 billion for EleutherAI's GPT-Neo-X
- [analysis] Christopher Manning — says he does not consider LLaMA's release an unacceptable risk and that it is often better to regulate uses of technology than its availability
- [analysis] Vipul Ved Prakash — calls the Senate letter a "misguided attempt at limiting access to a new technology" that is "full of typical straw-man concerns"
- [analysis] Vipul Ved Prakash — argues that spammers will not use LLaMA or other LLMs because much cheaper ways of generating spam exist
- [analysis] Vipul Ved Prakash — compares the letter's concerns to risks from programming languages, which society does not restrict despite malicious uses
- [forecast] Vipul Ved Prakash — warns that the AI safety discourse could squelch American innovation and hand the technology to a few companies that have shaped the debate
- [fact] Sharon Goldman — reports that Manning and Falcon both pointed to the Technology Innovation Institute's 40-billion-parameter Falcon model as a better-quality openly available model
- [analysis] Christopher Manning — says LLaMA's release made little difference to the rate of progress or LLM dissemination, given models like Falcon
- [analysis] Adam Thierer — describes the letter as a "nasty gram" that carries an implied "or else" without threatening formal regulation
- [forecast] Adam Thierer — expects lawmakers to pressure platforms that host open-source models, which he says is how open source gets regulated without formal regulation

## Key Quotes

> "Look, it's easy for both government officials and proprietary competitors to throw open source under the bus, because policymakers look at it nervously as something that's harder to control — and proprietary software providers look at it as a form of competition that they would rather just see go away in some cases." — Adam Thierer, innovation policy analyst at R Street Institute

> "As such, I do not consider their release an unacceptable risk. We should be cautious about keeping good technology from innovative companies and students trying to learn about and build the future. Often it is better to regulate uses of technology rather than the availability of the technology." — Christopher Manning, director of the Stanford AI Lab

> "It makes no sense to use a language model to generate spam. I helped create what is possibly the most widely deployed anti-spam system on the Internet today, and I can say with confidence that spammers won't be using LLaMA or other LLMs because there are significantly cheaper ways of creating spam messages." — Vipul Ved Prakash, co-founder and CEO of Together

> "What are they going to do? Tell the UAE they can't make the model public?" — William Falcon, CEO of Lightning AI

> "At some point, lawmakers will start to put more and more pressure on other providers or platforms who may do business with or provide a platform for open-source applications or models. And that's how you get to regulating open source without formally regulating open source." — Adam Thierer, innovation policy analyst at R Street Institute
