## Summary

InfoWorld reports that the [[OpenSourceInitiative]] (OSI) unveiled version 1.0 of the Open Source AI Definition (OSAID) at the All Things Open 2024 conference in Raleigh, North Carolina. The definition requires an [[OpenSourceAI|open source AI]] system to grant the freedoms to use, study, modify, and share it, and it describes a preferred form for modification made of data information, code, and parameters. Endorsers quoted in the article include Stanford's Percy Liang, Nextcloud's Frank Karlitschek, and [[Mozilla]]'s Ayah Bdeir. Info-Tech Research Group analyst Brian Jackson objects that the definition leaves copyrighted [[TrainingData|training data]] unaddressed and warns of harms from open models.

## Key Claims

- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — released OSAID v1.0 at the All Things Open 2024 conference in Raleigh, North Carolina, as the result of a year-long global community initiative
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — describes v1.0 in its FAQ as "the first stable version" of an effort to recreate for AI practitioners the collaboration the Open Source Definition enabled for software
- [fact] InfoWorld — reports that the definition came out of a co-design process involving more than 25 organizations, including Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, Intel, Samsung, the Mozilla Foundation, the Linux Foundation, the Apache Software Foundation, and the United Nations International Telecommunications Union
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — requires an open source AI system to be available under terms that grant the freedoms to use, study, modify, and share it for any purpose, criteria derived from the Free Software Definition
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — states that these freedoms apply both to a fully functional system and to its discrete elements, with access to the preferred form for modification as a precondition
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — specifies the data information, code, and parameters that make up the preferred form for modification of machine learning systems
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — states that the definition does not require a specific legal mechanism for keeping model parameters freely available
- [forecast] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — expects the legal mechanism for model parameters to become clearer once the legal system has more opportunity to address Open Source AI systems
- [fact] Percy Liang — welcomed that v1.0 requires at least the complete code for data processing to be open source, calling that code the primary driver of model quality
- [forecast] Percy Liang — expects more to say once people try to apply the definition to concrete models
- [fact] Carlo Piana — stated, as OSI board chair, that the co-design process was "well-developed, thorough, inclusive, and fair"
- [analysis] Carlo Piana — said the board is confident the definition meets the standards of the Open Source Definition and the Four Essential Freedoms
- [fact] InfoWorld — reports that Nextcloud, which introduced its own Ethical AI rating in early 2023, endorses the OSAID and intends to embed it into its specification
- [analysis] Frank Karlitschek — says big tech firms are trying to hijack the term open source AI
- [analysis] Brian Jackson — says the OSI's standards are clear and consistent with the previous standards for releasing open source software
- [analysis] Brian Jackson — argues such a standard is needed because training data, model weights, and new architectures are not covered by traditional open source software licenses
- [analysis] Brian Jackson — accepts that a model may withhold training data that is illegal to release, such as medical data, but argues this leaves copyright-protected content in training data unaddressed
- [analysis] Brian Jackson — warns of harms from open source AI, including deepfakes and "nudify" apps that generate fake nude images of real people
- [analysis] Brian Jackson — cites Internet Watch Foundation reports of dark web forums trafficking child sexual abuse material whose creators prefer open source image generation models
- [analysis] Brian Jackson — says open source models can be modified to help create deepfakes, tailor phishing messages, or search automatically for vulnerable users
- [analysis] Ayah Bdeir — says the definition's requirement of enough training-data information to recreate a substantially equivalent system goes further than many proprietary or ostensibly open source models do today
- [analysis] Ayah Bdeir — argues that insisting on an ideologically pristine gold standard that no model builder meets could backfire
- [fact] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — stated, as OSI executive director, that reaching v1.0 was a difficult journey marked by differing opinions and occasional heated exchanges, and that the results align with the expectations set at the start of the two-year process
- [forecast] [[StefanoMaffulli]] — expects the OSI to keep improving the definition over time with the broader Open Source community

## Key Quotes

> "Coming up with the proper open source definition is challenging, given restrictions on data, but I'm glad to see that the OSI v1.0 definition requires at least that the complete code for data processing (the primary driver of model quality) be open-source." — Percy Liang, director of the Center for Research on Foundation Models, Stanford University

> "The devil is in the details, so I'm sure we'll have more to say once we have concrete examples of people trying to apply this Definition to their models." — Percy Liang, director of the Center for Research on Foundation Models, Stanford University

> "The co-design process that led to version 1.0 of the Open Source AI Definition was well-developed, thorough, inclusive, and fair." — Carlo Piana, OSI board chair

> "Now, we see big tech firms trying to hijack the term open source AI. We fully endorse the creation of a clear definition of open source AI by the community to protect users and the market." — Frank Karlitschek, Nextcloud CEO and founder

> "I accept that reasoning, but it doesn't address the problem of copyright-protected content being included in the training data." — Brian Jackson, principal research director, Info-Tech Research Group

> "We've already seen real-world harm as a result of open source AI." — Brian Jackson, principal research director, Info-Tech Research Group

> "This view of AI training data in Open Source AI may not be a perfect place to be, but insisting on an ideologically pristine kind of gold standard that will not actually be met by any model builder could end up backfiring." — Ayah Bdeir, AI strategy lead, Mozilla

> "This is a starting point for a continued effort to engage with the communities to improve the definition over time as we develop with the broader Open Source community the knowledge to read and apply OSAID v.1.0." — [[StefanoMaffulli]], OSI executive director
