## Summary

Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller of the think tank Open Future assess version 1.0 of the [[OpenSourceInitiative]]'s Open Source AI Definition (OSAID), released the same week. They call it an important step toward a shared standard, whose main value is a clear line on what does not count as [[OpenSourceAI]] and a minimum floor for training-data transparency that exposes [[OpenWashing]]. They also call it conservative: it sets no standard for sharing weights, excludes responsible-AI licenses, and does not require [[TrainingData]] to be openly licensed. On that last point they argue OSI should have used a different name, matching what observers call [[OpenWeights]] models, and reserved "open source" for systems that include open data.

- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — released version 1.0 of the Open Source AI Definition in the week before 2024-11-01
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — judge the OSAID an important step toward a standard for openness in AI, whose main value is a clear stance on what does not count as open source AI
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that the OSAID carries extra weight because of the standing the OSI enjoys as steward of the Open Source Definition
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — observe that the tone of the policy debate shifted over the past year from the risks of uncontrolled spread toward the value of openly shared AI systems against concentrations of power
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — say that OpenAI and, more recently, Mistral first committed to openness and then moved toward increasing closure
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that [[Meta]] makes its models openly available under conditions that significantly curb reuse and without transparency about the training data
- [fact] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — report that the Linux Foundation's Model Openness Framework ranks machine learning models in three tiers of completeness and openness
- [fact] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — report that [[Mozilla]] proposed a Framework for Openness in Foundation Models
- [fact] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — report that the Digital Public Goods Alliance convened a Community of Practice on AI as a Digital Public Good, which published recommendations
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that defining openness in AI is not a matter of applying a software framework, because the standard must cover training data, code, model weights, and documentation
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — note that the copyright status of model weights is still poorly understood, which leaves unclear how they can be openly shared
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — adopted the OECD definitions of an AI system and of machine learning for the OSAID
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — requires that source code used to train and run the system be available under OSI-approved licenses
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — requires sufficiently detailed information about the data used to train the system
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — requires that model parameters, including weights, be made available
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — characterize the OSAID as a conservative standard that does not venture beyond well-established rules for sharing code
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — say the OSAID avoids setting a standard for sharing model weights, citing a lack of legal clarity
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — conclude that systems under responsible AI licenses fail the OSAID, because those licenses discriminate against certain forms of use
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that this position ignores the substantial share of permissively shared models released under responsible AI licenses, which may still be a meaningful form of openness
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — decided that training data need not be available under an open license for a system to count as open source
- [fact] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — report that the OSI based the data decision on a working group of volunteer model developers, who declared data is not the preferred form for modifying a system
- [analysis] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — argues that an open-data requirement would limit the OSAID's reach, since many datasets cannot be shared, for example for privacy reasons
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — judge the data-information requirement much weaker than an open-license requirement, but still a progressive position in the wider policy debate
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that the OSAID makes corporate open-washing easy to identify
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — point to a visible trend toward less training-data transparency, driven by competition concerns and copyright-liability exposure
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue the OSAID data requirements can serve as a reference point for the EU AI Act's training-data transparency requirement
- [fact] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — state that Open Future developed a blueprint for an AI Act transparency template with [[Mozilla]] and several open source AI developers
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that open resources alone lack the volume and diversity to train large foundation models, so almost all open model efforts depend on web-scraped public data
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — judge the lack of an open-data requirement the correct choice from the AI developers' perspective
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — report that Open Data advocates see the missing open-data requirement as a form of open washing, and that some open source developers also contested the decision
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — frame the data choice as one between a stronger but narrower standard and a weaker but broader one, and say the OSI chose the latter
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that a tiered standard like the Model Openness Framework would resolve the data question most easily
- [fact] [[OpenSourceInitiative]] — opted for a binary, non-tiered standard, arguing it must be clear which systems comply
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue the OSAID missed a chance to acknowledge open data, for example as an optional way to meet the data transparency requirement
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that training datasets are the component that can best be understood as the source of an AI system, by analogy rather than direct equivalence
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — say the OSAID's name aligns it with the EU AI Act's definition of open source AI systems, which also does not require training data to be shared
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue it would have been preferable to name the definition after open weights models and reserve "open source" for AI systems that include open data
- [fact] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — report that [[Meta]], which calls its Llama models "open source," publicly declared that it does not agree with the definition
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — argue that Meta's objection shows the OSAID already pressures companies not to call partially open models "open source"
- [forecast] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — expect the OSI and its allies to face the challenge of turning the OSAID into a commonly accepted standard
- [forecast] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — hope the definition will be iterated, with open issues including legal means for sharing weights and the applicability of copyleft
- [fact] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — report that the Digital Public Goods Alliance announced an upcoming consultation on its standard for AI as a digital public good
- [analysis] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — call for a shared community norm that recognizes fully open AI systems with open datasets and supports a gradient of other data-sharing approaches
- [fact] Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller — state that Open Future and the OSI organized a workshop on data sharing and open source AI in mid-September 2024

## Key Quotes

> "(1) the complete description of all data used for training, including (if used) of unshareable data, disclosing the provenance of the data, its scope and characteristics, how the data was obtained and selected, the labeling procedures, and data processing and filtering methodologies; (2) a listing of all publicly available training data and where to obtain it; and (3) a listing of all training data obtainable from third parties and where to obtain it, including for fee." — [[OpenSourceInitiative]]

> "Ultimately, the choice regarding a data sharing requirement is between a stronger but narrower standard that secures various forms of openness of resources related to AI development and a weaker but broader standard that acknowledges that AI training is not just done with open data." — Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller, Open Future

> "Looking at the list of AI components and thinking about how models are trained on underlying data, training datasets are the primary component that can be understood as the source of AI systems." — Alek Tarkowski and Paul Keller, Open Future
